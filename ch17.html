Chapter 17

The Coach Who Wasn’t a Horse

Arrigo Sacchi sold shoes for his father’s factory in Fusignano and was never good enough to play for his village club. When Italy asked how a man with no playing career could possibly coach Milan, his answer gave this chapter its title. Between 1987 and 1991 he won two European Cups and buried the libero that had been Italy’s default for a quarter of a century.

Il gioco all’italiano

Milan’s European Cups in the sixties made the libero Italy’s default, and Juventus beating Liverpool at Heysel in 1985 confirmed it. A sweeper swept behind man-markers, and attacking was left to the intelligence of individuals and the creativity of the number 10. Liedholm had tried a zone at Roma and then Milan, but by Sacchi’s account it was really a mixed zone: you started in a zone, then man-marked inside it.

Milan, the shape Sacchi inherited   Opposing forwards. The markers track their men; Baresi sweeps behind.

A jockey, not a horse

Sacchi had taken Parma up from Serie C1 and, in 1987, beaten Milan twice in the Coppa Italia. Silvio Berlusconi, the club’s new owner with dreams of greatness, hired him. A former shoe salesman in charge of Gullit and van Basten faced an obvious credibility problem, and he answered it tartly.

‘A jockey doesn’t have to have been born a horse.’

Gullit and van Basten arrived that summer, Rijkaard the next. And, radically for Italy, the back four played not behind a libero but in a line.

The three Dutchmen are ringed. The gold band is the line Baresi stepped up into.

The short team

Sacchi’s key idea was compression: no more than twenty-five metres between the last defender and the centre-forward. Behind the block sat an aggressive offside trap; in front of it, three barriers to be broken in quick succession. Moving as one unit, up and down and from side to side, Milan reached the ball first and tired less. With the ball, Sacchi wanted five players ahead of it and a man wide on each flank.

The shaded band is the 25-metre block, seen from the side.

A sliding arc, not a flat line

The back four was a sliding arc that lay flat only when the ball was in the centre of the field. As the ball travels across the pitch the whole eleven shifts with it: the ball-side player steps up, the far side drops to cover, and if one full-back goes forward the other ten adjust. Maldini said Milan won matches through movement off the ball rather than through the individual duel.

Drag to orbit. Watch the back four tilt as the ball crosses the field.

Four reference points, three kinds of pressing

Out of possession every player read four things: the ball, the space, the opponent and his teammates. Pressing, for Sacchi, was about controlling space rather than running, and it was psychological as much as physical. Milan varied it: partial pressing that jockeyed, total pressing that hunted the ball, and fake pressing, a show of intent that bought time to recover.

The caption over the far half cycles through the three modes.

Five against ten

To convince Gullit and van Basten that five organised players would beat ten disorganised ones, Sacchi staged a drill: Galli, Tassotti, Maldini, Costacurta and Baresi against ten attackers for fifteen minutes. The only rule was that if the ten lost the ball they restarted from ten metres inside their own half. Sacchi ran it constantly, and the ten never scored.

The gold line is the restart line. Interceptions light up the defender.

Shadow play

Before the 1989 semi-final, Real Madrid sent a scout to watch Milan train. He reported eleven players on a full-sized pitch playing against nobody, without a ball. Sacchi would call out where the imaginary ball was and the side moved like clockwork around it. Shadow play had been routine in England since the sixties; on the continent it was a revolution.

Five past Real Madrid

1–1 in the Bernabéu. For the second leg Leo Beenhakker started the sprinter Paco Llorente, which thinned his midfield: Schuster could not get near Rijkaard and Ancelotti, and Butragueño was dragged right to help out, breaking his partnership with Hugo Sánchez. Ancelotti’s thirty-yard drive began it. The fourth belonged to the three Dutchmen: Gullit heading down Rijkaard’s pass for van Basten to crash it into the top corner. 5–0, and Steaua were beaten 4–0 in the final.

The trap that failed

Sacchi took the national job in 1991 and found he could no longer school players every day. Italy against Norway, USA 94: Benarrivo replaced Tassotti. Baresi and Costacurta stepped up on the Norwegian centre-forward; Benarrivo, unused to playing with them, held his ground and played the runner onside. Pagliuca came out, fouled outside the box and was sent off. To get a keeper on, Sacchi took off Roberto Baggio, who stared at the bench as if the coach had gone mad.

Italy   Norway. The gold line is where Benarrivo stayed.

Capello’s Milan

Capello inherited the machine and made it more cautious: the same 4-4-2, the same pressing, the same back four of Tassotti, Baresi, Costacurta and Maldini, but with an out-and-out holder, Marcel Desailly, in front of them, something Sacchi’s doctrine of universality forbade. Milan took the 1994 scudetto scoring thirty-six goals in thirty-four games and conceding fifteen. Then, in Athens, without the suspended Baresi and Costacurta, they took Cruyff’s Barcelona apart 4–0.

The ringed player is the holder Sacchi would never have used.

Born in the brain

Sacchi was never convinced by Capello’s side, and Maldini agreed that the 1989 team was the best he ever played in. Sacchi wanted intelligent players rather than solo artists, an orchestra rather than robots, and the morning after Steaua he woke with a sweetness he never tasted again. The libero was gone; the line and the zone were Italy’s new default.
