Chapter 4

How Fascism Destroyed the Coffee House

England answered the 1925 offside law with Chapman's third back and then pretended nothing had changed. Central Europe kept the pyramid and argued about it in the coffee houses of Vienna, where Hugo Meisl's Wunderteam turned passing into an aesthetic. It was Vittorio Pozzo's Italy, athletic, man-marking and backed by Mussolini, that won the World Cups of 1934 and 1938, and the Anschluss finished what the results had begun.

The plaster cast

Chapman solved one problem for one club, and English football copied the fix without absorbing the idea. When the FA made shirt numbers compulsory in 1939 it numbered the 2-3-5 as though it were still universal: 2 and 3 at the back, 4-5-6 in the halves, 7 to 11 up front. Teams playing the W-M therefore lined up 2-5-3, 4-6, 8-10, 7-9-11, and the number 5, the old centre-half, sat between the full-backs. That is why the British still call a centre-back a "centre-half". Newspapers printed pyramid line-ups into the 1960s; Peter Doherty's Doncaster won matches simply by swapping shirts.

Willy Meisl: "the plaster cast remained, no soccer sorcerer or professor was here to smash it"
England, numbered as the FA insisted

The Ring Café

In Britain football was argued over in the pub; in Vienna, Budapest and Prague it was theorised in the coffee house. Each club had its own café, and the Ring Café became a kind of parliament for the whole Viennese game, where a largely Jewish bourgeoisie did for tactics what it did for music and theatre. Its hero was Matthias Sindelar of Austria Vienna, a centre-forward so slight he was called Der Papierene, the Paper-man, who drifted out of position and improvised where Rapid's bullish Josef Uridil had simply charged. Hugo Meisl distrusted him until 1931. Then he could no longer leave him out.

Torberg on Uridil and Sindelar: "as different from each other as a tank from a wafer"
Austria's Wunderteam

The Danubian Whirl

With Sindelar installed, Austria beat Scotland 5–0 in May 1931 and went unbeaten through their next eleven matches, nine of them won, with forty-four goals along the way and the Dr Gerö Cup at the end of it. The shape was the classic 2-3-5 with Josef Smistik as an elegant attacking centre-half, but the centre-forward refused to be a fixed point: he dropped, the inside-forwards ran beyond him, the wingers cut in, and the ball moved in short passes through the gaps he opened. The rotation became known as the Danubian Whirl. It was Jimmy Hogan's ball control raised into a style, and the coffee houses saw in it their own way of thinking made flesh.

Austria Scotland

London, December 1932

England were not the best team in the world, but they were unbeaten at home against foreign sides, and all Vienna stopped to listen: crowds in the Heldenplatz, the parliamentary finance committee adjourned. Austria fell two behind to Jimmy Hampson, then out-passed England for an hour. What baffled the hosts was the Austrian habit of dropping every player behind the ball when they lost it, then spinning webs of passes as they came forward again. Zischek and Sindelar scored, but the webs rarely reached the goal. England won 4–3; The Times gave Austria the moral victory and filed the result under continental teams lacking punch.

England, W-M Austria, 2-3-5

Vienna, May 1936

Meisl finally beat England in Vienna, in heat that made English charging unsustainable. When Hogan questioned the stamina of the inside-forwards, Meisl said he would take a decisive lead early and defend it, and did. The key was Sindelar drifting away from the England centre-half John Barker. In a W-M the stopper marks the number 9, so Barker followed him toward the halfway line and the space behind him opened for the inside-forwards. Seventeen years later Nándor Hidegkuti would do the same to Harry Johnston at Wembley. Austria won 2–1, and Jack Crayston admitted England had not known whether they were coming or going.

Pozzo's centro mediano

Vittorio Pozzo learned his football in Bradford and Manchester, worshipping Charlie Roberts, a centre-half who swept long passes out to the wings. He loathed the third-back game, yet Italy lacked a creator good enough for a pure 2-3-5. His answer was Luisito Monti, the heavy Argentine oriundo nicknamed Doble ancho for the ground he covered. Monti played as a centro mediano, a halfway house: when Italy lost the ball he dropped to mark the opposing centre-forward; when they had it he stepped up and dispatched it wide. Because he sat deeper, the two inside-forwards withdrew to support the wing-halves. The shape was a 2-3-2-3, a W-W, which Pozzo called the metodo.

Metodo versus sistema

Both systems were answers to the same offside law, and both were reached from the pyramid. Chapman pulled the centre-half back between the full-backs to make three defenders, then withdrew the inside-forwards to fill the midfield he had emptied: the W-M, which Pozzo called the sistema. Pozzo kept two full-backs and let his centre-half drop only halfway, so the team stayed a 2-3 at the back with a W in front of it. Mario Zappa of La Gazzetta dello Sport thought the metodo synthesised the best of every system; Glanville reckoned Italians only grasped the full implications of the W-M in 1939. Same eleven, one man's depth apart.

Italy, metodo England, sistema

Rome, 1934

The home World Cup turned Pozzo's pragmatism into something harder. Italy drew 1–1 with Spain and won a brutal replay in which goalkeeper Ricardo Zamora had been kicked out of the tournament, then met Austria in the semi-final. Monti marked Sindelar out of the game; Austria did not manage a shot in the first forty minutes and lost 1–0 to a scrambled Guaita goal. Czechoslovakia led the final through Puč until Orsi's swerving equaliser, and Schiavio won it in extra time. Pozzo's man-marking rested on a simple idea: cut off the head that thinks for the other ten, and the whole system collapses.

John Langenus, referee: "beside the will to win all sporting considerations were non-existent"
Italy Austria

Paris, 1938

Italy retained the trophy with what Pozzo thought his best side, built again on solidity: attack with as few men as possible and never distract the half-backs from defending. Austria no longer existed; a combined German team went out to Switzerland in the first round. Hungary carried the Danubian style to the final, where Michele Andreolo, Monti's successor as centro mediano, checked György Sárosi while Italy's pace and fitness made the passing game look slow and old-fashioned. Italy won 4–2. Franco's Spain was travelling the same road, recasting la furia as a martial virtue. Football under fascism had become a matter of strength.

The Anschluss and the Paper-man

The Danubian style might have faded anyway as Sindelar aged and Meisl grew old, but the Anschluss made sure of it, scattering the Jewish intelligentsia that had sustained the coffee house. On 3 April 1938 Sindelar played for an Ostmark XI against Germany in a so-called reconciliation game, scored in a 2–0 win, and then declined to play for Sepp Herberger's united team. On 23 January 1939 he was found dead in a flat on Annagasse beside his girlfriend, killed by carbon monoxide from a faulty heater. The evidence says accident; Vienna preferred a martyr. Alfred Polgar wrote that he had followed his city to its death.

"To its end, the football of the coffee house remained heroically romantic."
