Chapter 15

The English Pragmatism (2)

‘As so often, progress began with defeat.’ A European Cup exit sends Liverpool’s boot-room towards patient passing; a few years later Watford and Wimbledon sprint the other way, and Charles Hughes turns the long ball into Football Association doctrine. The chapter follows that doctrine out to Sweden and Norway, and back to Wembley, where it takes its revenge on Graham Taylor.

Anfield, 7 November 1973

The night before, Miljan Miljanić’s Crvena Zvezda had held the ball, frustrated Liverpool and struck twice on the break through Lazarević and Janković to win 4–2 on aggregate. The next day six men (Shankly, Paisley, Fagan, Moran, Saunders and Bennett) met in the cramped boot-room to ask why a side imperious at home looked so vulnerable in Europe.

‘We realised it was no use winning the ball if you finished up on your backside.’ Bob Paisley
Liverpool Crvena Zvezda

Control and pass, control and pass

The stopper centre-half was declared extinct. When Larry Lloyd ruptured a hamstring, Phil Thompson, a midfielder, was pushed back beside Emlyn Hughes and Liverpool learned to build from the back ‘in little groups’, shifting the opposition’s pattern until Ray Kennedy or Terry McDermott could sneak in for the final pass. Four European Cups between 1977 and 1984 followed.

‘You might not be getting very far, but the pattern of the opposition is changing. Finally, somebody will sneak in.’ Bill Shankly

Clough’s Forest: one winger wide, one tucked in

Brian Clough scorned ‘Subbuteo men being pushed around a felt pitch’, but his 4-4-2 was precisely balanced. John Robertson created from the left; Martin O’Neill tucked in on the right so McGovern and Bowyer could shuffle across; Viv Anderson overlapped to supply the width; Garry Birtles held the ball up for Woodcock or Francis. Ball retention came first. Forest are still the one club with more European Cups than domestic titles.

‘A team blossoms only when it has the ball. Flowers need the rain.’ Brian Clough
Nottingham Forest

Graham Taylor’s Watford: press the ball wherever it is

At the same time another strand of English football ran the opposite way. Graham Taylor, at twenty-seven the youngest fully qualified FA coach in the country, had read about Viktor Maslov in the FA’s coaching magazine and built Watford on pressing. Elton John asked for Europe within five years; three promotions and a second-place finish in 1983 delivered it.

‘Even if the opposition right-back had the ball deep in his own half, we still pressed him… I knew we couldn’t defend our way into Europe.’ Graham Taylor
Watford Opposition

A 4-4-2 that looked like a 4-2-4

Shape mattered less than method. With Wilf Rostron and David Bardsley pushing on from full-back and real wingers in Nigel Callaghan and John Barnes playing high, the 4-4-2 came to resemble the 4-2-4 of the 1958 Brazilians; in 1982–83 Watford sometimes played a 3-4-3. The scores went with it: 8–0 against Sunderland, 4–4 and then 4–5 with Everton, 7–3 to Forest in the League Cup.

‘Because we kept going forwards, the opposition kept going back… We kept on posing questions.’ Graham Taylor

The long pass, the ‘reacher’ and the second ball

Charles Reep measured Watford in ‘reachers’, balls landing in the final third: 156 a game on average, a record 202 against Chelsea in February 1982 (later beaten by John Beck’s Cambridge with 219). Taylor’s answer was that his side’s real strength was winning the ball back in that third, which Reep’s system did not count. Reep refused to change and the association ended: ‘With Reep it was all or nothing.’

‘If Glenn Hoddle played one it was a long pass, but if Ian Bolton did it, it was a long ball… for accuracy in his long passing I’d take Bolton every time.’ Graham Taylor
Final third

Wimbledon: ‘as soon as they get it they just hit it’

Back in the Fourth Division, Dave Bassett began the season with a sweeper. When results fell away in November he changed ‘to get the ball up to the front very fast’. Wimbledon rose to sixth in the First Division in 1987 and won the FA Cup in 1988 under Bobby Gould, beating Liverpool; their players credited Vinnie Jones’s first-minute foul on Steve McMahon. The crowds stayed away. Wilson’s verdict: not pragmatic but nihilistic.

‘A poor man’s Watford when four front men adopt a cavalry charge formation in pursuit of high passes slung from behind.’ John Vinicombe, Brighton Evening Argus
Wimbledon

Charles Hughes and The Winning Formula

As the FA’s director of coaching from 1983 to 1994, Hughes made direct play official doctrine. From 202 goals in 109 hand-picked matches he found 87 per cent came from five passes or fewer, and that 52 per cent followed possession regained in the attacking third against 18 per cent in the defensive third. Wilson’s objections: 91.5 per cent of all moves are three passes or fewer anyway; the sample was tiny and selective (only ten Dutch goals); and shots may be the result of dominance, not its cause. Where Hughes had a point was pressing.

‘He believes that footballs should come down with icicles on them.’ Brian Clough
Defensive third Attacking third

Found out: Sparta Prague, 7–2

Watford’s first European season brought the answer Taylor had expected far earlier: sides that sat deep, held the ball and picked them off. Kaiserslautern and Levski Sofia were beaten; Sparta Prague were ‘men against boys’. Direct, pressing football takes a team only so far. Against opponents technically good enough to keep the ball under pressure, and in tournament heat, its deficiencies show. Hence the Euro 92 midfield of Daley, Webb, Palmer and Sinton, and no World Cup in 1994.

‘When you gave the ball away, they didn’t give it back to you.’ Graham Taylor
Watford Sparta Prague

Exported: Houghton, Hodgson and Sweden’s libero war

Allen Wade’s FA courses produced Bobby Houghton and Roy Hodgson, who took a zonal back four, a high offside line, hard pressing and long passes in behind to Malmö and Halmstads, against Lars Arnesson’s German-style libero. Five titles in six years and a European Cup final followed, but so did Sweden’s 1978 World Cup flop, and on 11 December 1980 the SvFF banned ‘the English style’ outright. Sven-Göran Eriksson’s Gothenburg won the 1982 Uefa Cup with it anyway.

‘Instead of… a libero who stays in his penalty area and a centre-forward who never tracks back, we set up a system of zonal defence, a back four, people pushing up.’ Roy Hodgson
Malmö / Halmstads Libero side

Drillo’s Norway: the ball into the ‘bakrom’

Egil Olsen concluded that where the ball is matters more than who has it: you are likelier to score, he argued, while the other goalkeeper holds it than while yours does. His 4-5-1 played forward the instant an opponent was off balance, often to Jostein Flo, a target-man stationed wide to out-jump the full-back at the back post. Norway reached two World Cups and, briefly, second in the world rankings. Against Brazil in 1998 they played 65 per cent of their passes forward; Brazil 35.

‘Å være best uten ball’: to be the best without the ball.
Norway The bakrom

Random chance has its revenge

Norway beat England 2–0 in Oslo in 1993 (‘do I not like that’), but the costlier result was the 1–1 at Wembley, a game England controlled until, with fourteen minutes left, the holding midfielder Kjetil Rekdal let fly from thirty yards and found the top corner. Did he think he would score, or was he just buying another ticket in Hughes’s raffle? Either way Taylor, who understood the method’s limits better than anyone, was left to reap the harvest Hughes had sown.
