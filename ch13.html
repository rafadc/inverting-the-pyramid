Chapter 13

Science and Sincerity

Kyiv, 1961. Dynamo have just won their first Soviet title and a twenty-two-year-old winger refuses to be congratulated, because sometimes they played badly and merely got more points than teams who played worse. Valeriy Lobanovskyi wanted proof, not luck. In the city that housed the USSR's first cybernetics institute he turned football into a system of twenty-two elements, and then into numbers. Two hundred and seventy miles away in Minsk, Eduard Malofeev wanted football of the heart.

The winger who wanted proof

Lobanovskyi the player was dilettantish, a dribbler who wanted to invent tricks and embarrass opponents, and he chafed against Viktor Maslov's strictures. But he had a gold medal in mathematics from high school and was studying heating engineering at the Kyivan Polytechnic Institute in the city that was the centre of the Soviet computer industry, home to the USSR's first cybernetics institute.

"A realised dream ceases to be a dream."

In him the great struggle between individuality and system was acted out. The player wanted to dribble; the engineer wanted to break the game into its component tasks.

Dynamo Kyiv, 1961 — Lobanovskyi on the left wing

A system of twenty-two elements

Football, he concluded, was a system of twenty-two elements, two sub-systems of eleven, moving within a defined area and subject to a series of restrictions, the laws of the game. If the two sub-systems were equal the outcome was a draw; if one was stronger, it won.

So much is obvious. The fascinating part was a peculiarity: the efficiency of the sub-system is greater than the sum of the efficiencies of its elements. Football was less about individuals than about the connections between them.

"All life is a number."
Each line is a connection between two elements of the same sub-system. The pulses are passes.

Coalitions

Playing for a poor Shakhtar side in 1968 he decided to quit rather than keep relying on luck: "It is necessary to create the ensemble, a collective of believers who subordinate themselves to the common playing idea." At Dnipro, from 1969, he began to build it, and in 1972 he met Anatoliy Zelentsov, a specialist in bioenergetics, who became inseparable from him.

Their most radical document was a list of twenty "coalition actions", from the setting of an offside trap to the creation of overlaps. A coalition is a small group whose combined output exceeds what its members could manage alone.

"To attack, it is necessary to deprive the opponent of the ball. When is it easier to do that – with five players or with all eleven? The most important thing in football is what a player is doing on a pitch when he's not in possession of the ball."

The team of four and the sheet on the wall

Lobanovskyi returned to Kyiv late in 1973 as one quarter of a staff. He modelled playing systems; Zelentsov ran the individual preparation of players; Oleh Bazylevych did the actual coaching; Mykhaylo Oshemkov handled "informational support", the collation of statistical data from games.

The morning after every match the breakdown was pinned to the notice-board. Each element of the game had a target set by the model, and the players could no longer disagree with the coach's eye.

"If a midfielder has fulfilled sixty technical and tactical actions in the course of the match, then he has not pulled his weight. He is obliged to do a hundred or more."
Numbers are technical-tactical actions per player. The one in red has not pulled his weight.

Squeezing: the zonal trap

Like Michels's Ajax, Lobanovskyi's Dynamo could press, penning opponents in and winning the ball high up the pitch. The principle was spatial: keep the preferred playing area as large as possible in possession and as small as possible while the opponent has the ball. "It's necessary to force the opponent into the condition you want them to be in. One of the most important means of doing that is to vary the size of the playing area."

The block shifts towards the ball as one, four players close the zone around it and the passing lanes out of it, and the interception is followed not by a clearance but by distribution. Four of the fourteen defensive tasks on the training-ground wall were about what to do once the ball had been won.

Model 1, "squeezing": pressing only in the opponent's half. Target: 130 short forward passes, 80 interceptions, 50 tackles per game.

Counter-attacking: the small area

Equally, they could sit deep and strike on the counter. It all depended on circumstance, but one thing stayed constant: "When we possess the ball we are attacking; when our opponents possess the ball we are defending. From this fundamental, football strategy is derived: how, where and when to attack or defend."

Sitting deep, the block is small and the opponent is invited into it. Once the ball is won the playing area becomes as large as possible, and the space ahead belongs to the sprinter Oleh Blokhin, European Footballer of the Year in 1975, the year Dynamo won the Cup-Winners' Cup for the first time.

Model 2, "counter-attacking": pressing only in own half. Target: 80 short forward passes, 110 interceptions, 70 tackles, 50 long forward passes per game.

Home and away: two modes

Preparation ran on three levels: individual technique, tactics for each specific opponent, and a strategy for the competition as a whole, which acknowledged that no side could stay at maximum for a season. So Dynamo lost late-season games with the title won, and habitually killed games away from home, playing for a draw and conserving energy.

Their own example was the 1977 European Cup quarter-final against Bayern Munich. Away, the model was neutralisation and a draw; they lost 1–0. In Kyiv they chose a model based on squeezing and fighting for the ball in Bayern's half, seeking numerical advantage in various areas, and won 2–0.

The same eleven switch between the squeezing and counter-attacking models. Watch the size of the block.

Universality

The goal was what Lobanovskyi called universality: forwards who defend and backs who attack. There was no contradiction, because attacking and defending were related not to position on the pitch but to possession.

"No other coach ever demanded that I should chase opponents even back into my own penalty box." — Serhiy Yuran

Critics said he stifled individuality. Rather, he made players aware that individual skill was only of use within the system. "The tactics are not chosen to suit the best players. They must fit our play." One per cent talent, 99 per cent hard work.

The forward tracks back to his own box; once the ball is won, the full-back overlaps to the byline.

A line of backs: Lyon, 1986

Set moves were practised, to be used, Zelentsov said, not robotically but like a chess player adapting gambits to circumstance. The classic example was Dynamo's second goal in the 3–0 Cup-Winners' Cup final win over Atlético Madrid in 1986, their second European trophy.

Vasyl Rats advanced down the left, drew two men and played inside to Ihor Belanov. Two touches, and as the centre-back came across he laid it right, without a glance, for Vadym Yevtushenko, who took one pace, forced the left-back inside and flicked it right for the overlapping Blokhin to lift over the goalkeeper. Less football than a rugby team working the ball along a line of backs until the overlap appears.

Atlético Madrid   Dynamo Kyiv

The hive: the USSR, 1986 and 1988

With the national team he demanded "a star-team, not a team of stars", and in 1975 twice fielded an XI made up entirely of Dynamo players. His squad went on strike at the 1976 Olympics, exhausted. Recalled on the eve of the 1986 World Cup, he hammered Hungary 6–0, then lost 4–3 to Belgium in one of the great World Cup games. "As a coach you can't account for individual errors."

At Euro 88 the USSR beat the Netherlands and England and outplayed Italy 2–0; Enzo Bearzot spoke of "modern football at 100km/h". But the sweeper Oleh Kuznetsov was booked and missed the final.

"Have you seen how bees fly? There is a leader. The leader turns right and all the hive turn right. Every team has players who link coalitions." — Zelentsov

Without their leader the USSR missed a penalty, suffered Marco van Basten's volley and lost 2–0.

Sincere football

The opposition came from Minsk. Eduard Malofeev, loquacious and ebullient, thanked God each morning and jumped up and down to celebrate being alive. His Dinamo Minsk played what he called "sincere football": no bumping, no barging, no paying referees, just attacking. "Football of the heart, not of the head." Its hero was Alexander Prokopenko, a shy, alcoholic, instinctive genius who drank with the fans and would sooner die than stop running.

In 1982 it worked. Prokopenko's backheel against Dynamo Kyiv became the goal of the season, and Minsk clinched the title 4–3 at Spartak after a team-talk about monkeys sacrificing themselves to lions. Lobanovskyi pointed out that the backheel game ended in a draw, a valuable away point for Kyiv.

"In my life I have seen many things, but never sincere football."
Dinamo Minsk pour forward; Kyiv sit deep for the point.

"Everybody plays a pressing game now"

Malofeev took the USSR to the brink of Mexico 1986 and was replaced by Lobanovskyi before the tournament began. Lobanovskyi's Dynamo, tenth in 1984 ("a path always remains a path"), did the double in 1985 and added the Cup-Winners' Cup; he won eight Soviet titles in all, then returned in 1996 to build a third great team around Andriy Shevchenko that reached the 1999 Champions League semi-final.

By his death in 2002 players had "a greater freedom and an individuality" that a word or a glance could no longer command. But the system outlived the man. As Marcello Lippi put it: everybody plays a pressing game now.

The whole block follows the ball as a single organism.
