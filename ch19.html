Chapter 19

Ghosts and the Machine

By the mid-nineties pressing and the cult of speed seemed to have killed the fantasista as surely as they had killed the winger. Instead the playmaker was hunted down by pairs of holding midfielders, reinvented inside the 4-2-3-1 and its double pivot, and finally dissolved into a game with no fixed lines. This is the chapter where the specialist gives way to the universal player.

Euro 2000: the artists come back

The mid-nineties looked bleak for the fantasista: five-man midfields, muscle over finesse, Brazil winning the 1994 World Cup with two destroyers and a penalty shoot-out. Then came Euro 2000, arguably the best tournament of the modern era. Germany, still built around the back five that Dieter Eilts had shielded in 1996, went home without a win. France put Djorkaeff, Zidane and Dugarry behind Henry; the Netherlands and Portugal packed in three creators apiece. Italy took another road, the "broken team": an attacking three excused defensive work and a defensive seven behind them, as in Zaccheroni's 1998 Milan of Weah, Bierhoff and Leonardo. It made the playmaker exalted, necessary and, in the end, impossible.

Germany 1996, then France 2000, then Zaccheroni's Milan

The enganche, and the two men sent to stop him

In Argentina the playmaker is the enganche, the hook, who lives between midfield and attack. Juan Carlos Lorenzo popularised the role with Onega in a 4-3-1-2 at the 1966 World Cup; Bochini, Maradona and finally Riquelme embodied it, the still point around whom everything turned. The trouble is that one creative outlet is easy to stifle, and modern shapes carry two holding midfielders at little attacking cost. Kanu was smothered by Touré and Zokora; at the 2006 World Cup Pékerman pulled Riquelme after seventy-two anonymous minutes against Germany and sent on Cambiasso, switching to 4-4-2. Stop the designated No. 10 and the creativity almost dries up.

"Nobody wants playmakers, nobody buys them."
Argentina   Germany 2006

The width problem

Shapes built around a single playmaker, 4-3-1-2, the diamond, 3-4-1-2, funnel everything down the middle, so a defence can sit deep and allow only long shots. Bielsa's 3-3-2-2, with Verón and Ortega behind Batistuta and López, had the same fault until he pushed one forward and one trequartista out as wingers: the 3-3-1-3 gave his No. 10 options and baffled opponents, though it left Argentina open to the counter and out of the 2002 World Cup despite dominating possession. Yugoslavia found another fix against Finland that year. At half-time the flat diamond became a 3-4-1-2, wing-backs met the Finnish wingers, a spare man appeared at the back and in midfield, and 0-0 became 2-0.

Croatia's three roads

Croatia tried everything. In 1998 Blažević squeezed Boban, Prosinečki and Asanović into one midfield behind a back three of ball-playing stoppers and reached a World Cup semi-final. By 2006 Zlatko Kranjčar had followed Italy: to field his son Niko as a pure playmaker he needed Tudor and Kovač holding, which meant seven defenders and stodgy, predictable football. Bilić ended the debate at a stroke with four at the back, Kovač deep, Kranjčar wide, Srna on the other flank and Modrić in the middle. Modrić, as slight as any old No. 10, also organised and tracked back: the new kind of playmaker, robust and disciplined, and later a regista himself.

"the most creative midfield ever"

Why three at the back died

Bilardo's back three was two markers on two centre-forwards plus a sweeper. Against one striker it leaves two spare men, one useful and one wasted. Nelsinho Baptista's matching software shows the knock-on: a 3-5-2 facing a 4-5-1 that becomes 4-3-3 must send its wing-backs to the wingers, so five men mind three forwards; midfield is three against three; and up front two strikers face four defenders, one of whom can step into midfield and make it four against three while keeping three against two behind. Egypt's 3-4-1-2 thrived only because Africa still played 4-4-2; a lone striker in the 2008 final left Wael Gomaa wandering like a spare part.

"There's no point having three defenders covering one centre-forward."
Team A, 3-5-2   Team B, 4-3-3

The creator who drops off

British 4-4-2 pairs were either big man and quick man or creator and goalscorer, and the creator always drifted deeper. Cantona and Zola bewildered English centre-backs between the lines as Sindelar and Hidegkuti had; nobody called Sunderland's 1990 promotion side a 4-4-1-1, but with Gates behind Gabbiadini that is what it was, and Messi, a winger who cut infield, later became the image of the false nine. Push the wingers high, drop one striker off, and 4-4-2 is already 4-2-3-1: Manchester United beat Barcelona in the 1991 Cup-Winners' Cup final with Robson and Ince holding, Sharpe and Phelan wide and McClair off Hughes. Watch the forward drop, drag a centre-back out, and a runner attack the space.

Manchester United 1991   Barcelona

El doble pivote

In Spain 4-2-3-1 was common by 2000 and a default soon after. Once the playmaker became a second striker, a holding midfielder was detailed to pick him up (the late-nineties boom in the Makélélé role), so the deep forward drifted wide; if the holder followed, a second man dropped in as cover. Juanma Lillo, later Guardiola's mentor, was the first to use the shape deliberately, at Cultural Leonesa in 1991-92 with Sami and Teófilo Abajo as his pivots. Salamanca's players stared at it as if at a dinosaur, then won promotion. Irureta's Deportivo won the 2000 title with it, and a 3-2 home defeat by Real Madrid persuaded Ferguson his 4-4-2 was finished in Europe.

Pressuring to play

Lillo's 4-2-3-1 was above all a pressing shape, the most symmetrical way he could find of playing with four forwards. With the striker and the three behind him high, the midfield and the defence can play high too, so the whole team squeezes the pitch and tries to steal the ball near the opposition goal. It needs mobile players who can pass once they have won it, because the point of the pressing is the possession that follows. The pair of pivots step onto the opposing midfield, the back four sit near halfway and the front four hunt the ball-carrier. By the 2010 World Cup 4-2-3-1 had replaced 4-4-2 as football's default formation.

"They're pressuring to play, not playing to pressure."
4-2-3-1 pressing   Opponents building from the back

The 4-5-1 family and the end of the lines

4-5-1 was long dismissed as the right of the weak, but it is a family, not a formation. Swap an attacking midfielder for a holder and you have the Christmas tree: Jacquet's 1998 France freed Zidane behind Deschamps, Petit and Karembeu, with Djorkaeff alongside and the derided Guivarc'h holding the ball up; Ancelotti's Milan played it with Pirlo as regista between Gattuso and Ambrosini. Mourinho's Chelsea sat Makélélé in front of the back four, shuttled Lampard and Essien, and used Duff, Cole and Robben as wingers who doubled as midfielders: sometimes 4-1-2-3, sometimes 4-1-4-1, called 4-3-3. Looser offside laws stretched the effective pitch from forty metres to sixty, and the three old bands dissolved.

"There are no lines any more."
France 1998, then Ancelotti's Milan, then Mourinho's Chelsea

Ghosts and the machine

Sacchi saw the designated holder as a symptom of weakness: reactive football that balances stars with specialists instead of multiplying everyone's qualities, as at the galáctico Real Madrid where Zidane, Raúl and Figo would not track back. Like Lobanovskyi he wanted universality, players positioned by team-mates, opponents and space rather than by job title, and Guardiola and Klopp have since pulled the game his way. Every position changed with the playmaker: a top side now needs a centre-back who can carry the ball into midfield, and the poacher has all but vanished. Owen's gift for the shoulder of the last defender was not enough by his mid-twenties. Drogba, Henry and Ronaldo are hybrids, and they must function within the system.

"In my football, the regista is whoever has the ball."
