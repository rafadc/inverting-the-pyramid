Chapter 14

Fly Me to the Moon

Mexico, 1970. Brazil prepare on a NASA training programme, play in a yellow made for colour television and win the World Cup with a side that was every formation and none. A Rio newspaper likened the victory to the moon landing. Wilson's point is the sting in the tail: it looked like the future, and it was the last of the old football. This chapter follows what came after, from the Dutch shock of 1974 to Sarrià in 1982, the day system won.

All of them and none of them

Was Brazil 1970 a 4-4-2? A 4-3-3? A 4-2-4, even a 4-5-1? Wilson's answer is that it was all of them and none: eleven players who complemented one another perfectly. In modern parlance it would probably be called a 4-2-3-1, but such subtleties meant nothing then. Only Italy, in the final, stood in the way, and art beat futebol de resultados 4–1.

Brazil, 1970. The shape cycles every few seconds.

The cobras pick the team

João Saldanha cruised through qualifying, then fell out with the generals (he dropped Dario, the president's favourite) and with Pelé, whom he wanted to track back. Mário Zagallo, a safe pair of hands, inherited a side largely selected by a committee of senior players, the cobras: Gérson, Pelé and Carlos Alberto. Piazza dropped in as the quarto zagueiro. Gérson was the regista, the deep-lying playmaker, and the nineteen-year-old Clodoaldo gave him muscle alongside.

Bright rings mark the three cobras; the shaded box is the double pivot.

Two pontas de lança

Could Pelé and Tostão really play together? Neither was a conventional centre-forward; both were pontas de lança. The solution was fluidity: Tostão would drop off, Pelé would become the centre-forward, and then they would swap back. The risk was an empty box, which is where Jairzinho came in.

The Hurricane and the shuffle

Gérson spent hours in training clipping diagonals for Jairzinho, recalibrating his left foot for the thin Mexican air. Jairzinho, Furacão, arrived late into the box and scored in every game of the finals. The space he left behind was filled by Carlos Alberto, an attacking right-back in the spirit of Nílton Santos, and the rest of the defence shuffled across to cover.

Rivellino, vaguely on the left

Two problems became one solution. Everaldo was a defensive left-back, which balanced the back four, but a flying left-winger in front of him risked reopening the flank Ghiggia had exploited in the 1950 final. So Rivellino, another ponta de lança, was stationed loosely on the left, drifting infield as a counterweight to Jairzinho's surges and shooting whenever he could.

The red strip is the flank the cobras were worried about.

Italy's staffetta

Ferruccio Valcareggi dared not field both his great playmakers at once, so he ran a relay, the staffetta: Sandro Mazzola for the first half, Gianni Rivera for the second. Against Brazil's abundance of creators the contrast could hardly have been more pronounced.

Italy, 1970. Watch the touchline on the left.

The fourth goal, 86th minute

Three–one up, with no thought of running down the clock. Clodoaldo dribbled through three Italians in his own half and fed Jairzinho, now appearing on the left. Facchetti checked his run; Jairzinho turned in and laid the ball to Pelé, who waited, then rolled it outside for Carlos Alberto to hit first time into the corner. Still regularly voted the greatest goal ever, and the end of football's innocence: in the heat and altitude, pressing was impossible, so for the last time in a major tournament there was space.

1974: white shirts closing in

The Netherlands beat Uruguay 2–0, Argentina 4–0 and Brazil 2–0: the pride of Conmebol outplayed, 8–0 on aggregate. Rivellino, moved infield to play as the regista, was hounded by a phalanx of white shirts every time he received the ball. Modern football, it seemed, had no room for the old midfield creator. In Montevideo a fuel-company manager called Sergio Markarián watched on television and decided to become a coach. In Brazil the game passed to the military's technocrats; Cláudio Coutinho promised "polyvalence" in 1978 and delivered physical preparation.

Menotti's 4-3-3: la nuestra, updated

Argentina answered the Dutch with nostalgia and a stopwatch. César Luis Menotti, the chain-smoking romantic, insisted that efficacy and beauty were not divorced, and trained his players to be precise at speed. His 1978 side had Américo Gallego as a classic number 5, Mario Kempes and Osvaldo Ardiles as modern inside-forwards, and Leopoldo Luque flanked by Bertoni and Ortiz: the old W attacking shape, updated. In homage to the Dutch, the squad was numbered alphabetically. The title came with viveza too: the Dutch bus's circuitous route, the fuss over van der Kerkhof's cast, and a 6–0 against Peru that has never shaken off suspicion.

1982: the magic quadrilateral

Telê Santana followed Zagallo's policy and let his players play. Cerezo and Falcão, two registas, sat behind Zico and Socrates, two trequartistas; Eder was an auxiliary centre-forward beside the lumbering Serginho; and the width came from Leandro and Júnior tearing up the flanks. A 4-2-2-2 that looked narrow to Europeans and created its own space by movement. Brazil beat the USSR, Scotland, New Zealand and the reigning champions Argentina, and needed only a draw against Italy.

Il gioco all'Italiana

Bearzot's Italy were no longer pure catenaccio. Like the Dutch and the Germans, Italian football had made the libero a rounded footballer: Gaetano Scirea, a converted inside-forward rather than a converted full-back like Picchi, who could step out from the back and make an extra midfielder when his side had the ball. Italy had drawn all three group games and squeezed past Cameroon on a single goal. Paolo Rossi, back from a match-fixing ban, looked far from his best.

Italy, 1982. Scirea steps out and drops back.

Sarrià, fifth minute

Bruno Conti was allowed to advance almost forty yards down the right, cut infield and released the attacking left-back Antonio Cabrini, whose cross was headed in by Rossi, repaying Bearzot's faith. Had Brazil scored first, Italy's system and mentality were not built for chasing a game. Instead the pattern was set.

Brazilian attacking, Italian resistance

Socrates equalised within seven minutes, one-two with Zico, drive past Zoff at the near post. Cerezo's careless square pass gave Rossi a second. Falcão's ferocious strike made it 2–2, which was all Brazil needed. They could have tightened up and held what they had, but that was not the Brazilian way. They kept attacking.

The third goal, and the day system won

A Conti corner was half-cleared, Tardelli half-hit his shot from the edge of the box, and Rossi, played onside by a dozing Júnior, hooked the ball past Waldir Peres. Zico called it "the day that football died". Wilson's reading is cooler: it was the day a certain naivety died, after which it was no longer possible to pick the best players and let them get on with it. Great individual talents still had their place, but inside something knowing, protected and covered for.

The red line is the offside line at the moment Tardelli shoots.

Statistics take over

Santana's style lasted until 1986 and a penalty defeat to France's carré magique. By 1990 Sebastião Lazaroni's Brazil had added a centre-back and played a 3-5-2 that suited their wing-backs and pretty much nobody else. A study by Murici Sant'anna found the ground covered by players doubled between the mid-seventies and the mid-nineties. The logic held: three straight World Cup finals from 1994 to 2002. But where 1958 had Didí and Zito in the centre of midfield, 2010 had Gilberto Silva and Felipe Melo, and the man who sees most of the ball had been told to destroy.
