Chapter 21

The Post-Cruyffian World

In April 2013 Bayern and Dortmund took Barcelona and Real Madrid apart with less of the ball, and the age of pure possession ended. What followed was not a new revolution but a synthesis: German pressing, the return of the back three, full-backs who became midfielders, and super-clubs too rich to fall. The pyramid, at last, stands on its point.

The night the pass stopped winning

On 23 April 2013 Bayern Munich beat Tito Vilanova’s Barcelona 4–0; the next night Klopp’s Dortmund beat Real Madrid 4–1. Both German sides had less of the ball, but this was not Inter in 2010 or Chelsea in 2012 sitting at the edge of their own box and hoping. They were quicker, stronger and sharper, hunting the ball in midfield and attacking at pace the instant they won it. Wilson reads the two nights as the end of the obsession with ball retention: the answer to a game built on keeping possession turned out to be a game built on regaining it, faster and more frenetic than Barça’s own press.

Barcelona   Bayern. First the block sits, then it steps up, wins the ball and goes.

Rangnick counts eleven

Germany came late to pressing. Its seventies possession sides never pressed, the libero stayed the playmaker into the nineties, and zonal marking was dismissed as foreign over-complication. In February 1983 the 24-year-old player-manager of sixth-tier Viktoria Backnang faced Lobanovskyi’s Dynamo Kyiv in a friendly and, at an early throw-in, counted the opposition because the pressing felt like twelve men. Ralf Rangnick, with the self-taught engineer Helmut Groß and Wolfgang Frank, who drilled a young Mainz centre-back called Jürgen Klopp on videos of Sacchi’s Milan, spent fifteen years spreading zone and press through the lower leagues. When Rangnick explained a flat back four on television in 1998 the establishment mocked him as ‘a professor’.

Viktoria Backnang   Dynamo Kyiv. Wherever the ball goes, the swarm follows.

Gegenpressing, the best playmaker

After the humiliations of 1998 and Euro 2000 the DFB built 121 regional centres, Klinsmann and Löw brought systematic pressing, and Jens Lehmann, then Manuel Neuer, replaced the immobile keeper with a sweeper. Between 2005 and 2010 the time a German player took between controlling the ball and passing it fell from 2.8 seconds to under one. Klopp, a television hit in the studio where Rangnick had been mocked, won two titles with Dortmund by winning the ball back high the moment it was lost, while the opponent was still disoriented. The idea diversified: Klopp’s sides encircled the ball, Heynckes’s Bayern marked the receivers, Nagelsmann’s Hoffenheim blocked the passing lanes.

‘Gegenpressing is the best playmaker in the world.’
Dortmund   Opponents. The caption cycles through the three variants.

Three soloists

Barcelona did not collapse when their style stopped being hegemonic; wealth insulated them, and they won the league in the season they lost 7–0 on aggregate to Bayern. Luis Enrique, a Sporting Gijón rather than La Masia graduate, was nearly sacked in January 2015. Then Messi moved back into the middle as a false nine, Suárez shifted right, and Barça won thirty-one of their last thirty-five games and the Champions League. Neymar and Suárez mattered because, against opponents happy to sit deep with 20 per cent of the ball, a dribble breaks a marking structure a pass cannot: Barça dribbled three times more often than in 2011, and Rakitić played the quick, risky pass Xavi never did.

Barcelona 2015   A deep block. Only the players Wilson names are labelled.

Five carriers: the back three returns

Spain went the same way. Van Gaal’s Netherlands beat them 5–1 in 2014 and Sampaoli’s Chile pressed them out of the World Cup; Conte’s Italy did it again at Euro 2016. All three used a back three. Xavi explained why: three defenders and two wide players give five men to carry the ball out, so a front three cannot press them all, while two strikers occupy both centre-backs and force a full-back to step out. Gasperini’s Atalanta pressed with five midfielders from the same base. For sides that wanted to press, or to escape a press, the back three that England had abandoned in 1963 was suddenly modern again.

‘It put us in great difficulty.’
Italy in a 3-5-2   Spain pressing. The ringed players are the five carriers.

Conte’s trapezium

Three-nil down at Arsenal in September 2016, Antonio Conte switched Chelsea to 3-4-2-1 at half-time. They won their next thirteen league games and the title, the first English champions to field a back three regularly since Catterick’s Everton in 1962–63. Sliding David Luiz between Azpilicueta and Cahill freed Moses and Alonso to play as midfielders; Kanté and Matić in front of the three recreated the trapezium of the W-M’s defensive M, doing the job wing-halves once did; Hazard and Pedro lived in the pocket between the opposing holders and full-backs. By spring, thirteen of the twenty Premier League clubs had tried a back three.

Chelsea   Arsenal. The gold outline is the trapezium; the shaded squares are the pockets.

Full-backs as midfielders

Once the back four had released the full-back, from Nílton Santos onwards, there was no putting him back. Guardiola took the idea further than anyone. At Bayern, Alaba and Lahm tucked inside to play as central midfielders, forming with the back three the same trapezium as the W-M; at City, Walker, Mendy and Delph came inside their wingers to attack the join between full-back and centre-back. Watch the 4-3-3 move: both full-backs step in and the shape in possession is two at the back, three across and five ahead of the ball, the pyramid this book started with; the holder drops and it is Chapman’s 3-2-2-3; the ball is lost and it is a 4-4-2 block.

Guardiola’s side. Lahm and Alaba are labelled and ringed as they tuck in.

Bypass the press

Guardiola’s Bayern and City were overwhelming at home yet kept collapsing in Europe: 4–0 to Real Madrid in 2014, three first-half goals to Klopp’s Liverpool in the 2018 quarter-final, two in minutes against Tottenham, Lyon and Real. The old answer to Guardiola was to sit deep and deny space, the way Inter, Chelsea, Mourinho and Simeone did. Klopp disliked the randomness of that and offered another: go through the press at pace and the high line, and the players behind it, panic. When he arrived at Liverpool in 2015 he was selling England a sharper version of its own eighties football. No manager has anything like his record against Guardiola, though City still won one of their 2017–18 meetings 5–0.

Manchester City   Liverpool. The gold line is City’s high line; the red band is the space behind it.

Qatar 2022: platforms for geniuses

Club and international football have become almost different games. National coaches lack the training time for the automatisms of a City or a Liverpool, so tournaments reward compact functionality and smart counters, as Croatia and Morocco showed, and punish an unrefined high line, as Flick’s Germany found. Both finalists were built around a genius who barely tracked back. Argentina became a platform for a 35-year-old Messi, who flitted through games yet decided all four knockout ties. Mbappé’s refusal to defend left France exposed on their left, so in the semi-final and final he was moved into the middle and the diligent Marcus Thuram took his flank.

‘In trying to kill the bad guys, we’ve killed the good guys, too.’
Argentina   France. The red patch is the flank Mbappé left open.

The pyramid, inverted

No side today drags football in a direction everyone must follow. Klopp bought Thiago for control; Guardiola bought Haaland for directness; the two extremes edged towards a synthesis. Back threes came and went, false nines are understood but rare, goalkeepers must pass, data promises to reshape everything, and players’ celebrity resists the systems that need them. Wilson calls the period retrenchment rather than radicalism. But the book’s arc is complete: from two defenders and five forwards to five defenders and one, each turn of the cycle carrying the memory of the last. Guardiola’s Barça felt like a pinnacle that will not soon be surpassed.

‘Many before have hailed the end of history; none have ever been right.’
