Chapter 20

Pressing and Possession

Wembley, May 2011: Barcelona pass Manchester United into submission, 3–1, and Alex Ferguson calls them the best team he has ever faced. Wilson traces how that side was built over forty years, from Vic Buckingham and Rinus Michels to Cruyff and Guardiola, and sets it against the pressing, periodised football of Mourinho's Porto and the reactive Inter that finally stopped it. Possession and pressing turn out to be two halves of one idea: having the ball, and getting it back.

La furia's last stand

Before Barcelona could become Barcelona they had to survive the final flowering of la furia. Javier Clemente's Athletic Bilbao were built around the bloque, two holding midfielders screening the back four, and a direct game that Clemente set against the passing he mocked as tiki-taka, a term he coined as an insult. His feud with César Luis Menotti's Barcelona, where Maradona and Schuster played, poisoned two seasons: Goikoetxea's tackle broke Maradona's ankle in September 1983, Athletic took the 1984 title by a point, and the Copa del Rey final ended in a mass brawl. No Spanish side since has won so much playing so physically.

Patapún y p'arriba: bish-bosh, up it goes.
Athletic Bilbao Barcelona

Twenty zones

Guardiola arrived in 2008 steeped in Cruyff and Van Gaal, and drilled his ideas on a training pitch sliced into twenty sections. The penalty-area lines run the full length of the field; the six-yard-box lines run between the two boxes to carve out the half-spaces; the wide channels are cut again between the eighteen-yard line and halfway. Players read the zone the ball is in and shift as a unit, and anyone may fill a zone the template gives to someone else, so long as the structure survives. The point is not decoration: a team that attacks in formation is already in shape to win the ball back.

Barcelona 2010–11 the zone the ball is in

Three across, two deep

The grid comes with a rule of thumb: never more than three players on the same horizontal line, never more than two on the same vertical one. If a fourth drifts into an occupied row, someone else moves automatically. That guarantees the man in possession two or three passing angles, which is what the endless rondos are for: knowing who is around you before the ball arrives. Guardiola says it takes about fifteen passes to build the right structure, and he bristles at the word tiki-taka, which to him means passing for its own sake. This is juego de posición: passing in order to arrange the team.

There is only one secret: I've got the ball or I haven't.
Barcelona passing triangles; the row flashes red when four share it

Five seconds

Possession was also Barcelona's defence. Like Van Gaal, Guardiola gave his players five seconds after losing the ball to win it back, hunting in packs because attacking in formation left them already close to it. If the counter-press failed, the team dropped into its defensive shape rather than chasing shadows. The idea was older than Pep: Terry Venables, who restored pressing at Camp Nou in 1985, wanted three or four players hustling the man in possession so the ball could be robbed where a quick counter-attack was on. Watch the swarm close, and then the retreat when the five seconds run out.

The false nine at the Bernabéu

Messi began under Guardiola on the right, a number ten asked to play wide for the good of the system. On 2 May 2009 he was moved into the middle against Real Madrid and kept dropping off Fabio Cannavaro and Christoph Metzelder to join Xavi and Iniesta, making three against Lassana Diarra and Fernando Gago at the base of Madrid's midfield. Neither centre-back followed him. Messi scored twice and set up Thierry Henry as Barcelona won 6–2 and, in effect, the title. From 2010–11, with David Villa and Pedro either side of him, the false nine was no longer an option but the default.

It's just not in our game to follow him into midfield.
Barcelona Real Madrid

Porto's pressing trap

The other great school of the era came from Portugal, where Vítor Frade's tactical periodisation trained the physical, technical, tactical and mental sides of the game in a single exercise, and José Mourinho made Frade director of methodology at Porto. Mourinho's Porto pressed high as a unit. If one opposing centre-back was uncomfortable on the ball, the pressure went onto his partner to force the weaker man to run with it; in the 2004 Champions League final Monaco were caught offside twelve times. Sessions rehearsed specific scenarios so precisely that Vítor Baía said it was as though the coach could see the future.

Porto 2004 Monaco

The anti-Barcelona

Passed over for the Barcelona job in 2008 in favour of a reserve-team coach, Mourinho, once the club's translator, decided that if they wanted the ball he would do without it. His Inter knocked Barcelona out of the 2010 Champions League at Camp Nou with nineteen per cent possession, packing men behind the ball while Walter Samuel and Lúcio dealt with Zlatan Ibrahimović, a striker who stayed where towering centre-backs like him. Wilson wonders how the tie would have gone with Messi dragging them into midfield instead. Mourinho's Madrid then ended Guardiola's reign by winning La Liga in 2012, but the more reactive he became, the further he drifted from the mainstream.

Three, two, one, none

Spain refined the Barcelona blueprint tournament by tournament. Luis Aragonés used three central forwards at the 2006 World Cup, Torres, Villa and Luis García; two at Euro 2008; Vicente del Bosque one in 2010, when every knockout game was won 1–0; and at Euro 2012 none, with Cesc Fàbregas as the most advanced player, picked to pass rather than finish. Del Bosque wanted control: five chances against none is safer than thirty against five. Opponents sat deep and denied Spain profundidad, until Italy pressed high in the final, the space behind them was exploited and Spain won 4–0. Menotti's bullfighter, not the bull.

Spain Italy, Euro 2012 final

The third year is fatal

Béla Guttmann warned that the third season is fatal, and Guardiola's fourth at Barcelona read like a Greek tragedy. Fearing predictability against deep defences, he switched to an aggressive Bielsa-style back three and pushed Dani Alves and everyone else higher to outflank blanket defences. It made Barcelona easier to mark: nobody arrived from deep at speed because everybody was already near the box. Against Chelsea in the 2012 semi-final the team went flat in the closing minutes, undone, as against Inter, by concentration, spirit and a little luck. Guardiola left having won fourteen of nineteen possible trophies, failing, if it was failure, on his own terms.

Barcelona 3-4-3 Chelsea

The great tradition

Barcelona did not simply happen. Wilson draws the line back to Queen's Park inventing the passing game in 1872, to Bob McColl carrying it to Newcastle in 1901, to Peter McWilliam at Tottenham, who coached Vic Buckingham, who laid foundations at Ajax and Barcelona for Rinus Michels and Johan Cruyff. Cruyff's Dream Team of 1992, with Koeman stepping out of defence and Laudrup as a prototype false nine, handed the philosophy on to La Masia and to Guardiola. By Sacchi's measure of greatness, a style remembered as vividly as the victories, Guardiola's Barcelona are the worthy heirs. There are many ways of playing, but this is the great tradition.
