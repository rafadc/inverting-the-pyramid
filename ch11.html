Chapter 11

After the Angels

Tactical shifts tend to be gradual. In Argentina one can be pinpointed to a single afternoon: Helsingborg, 15 June 1958, Czechoslovakia 6, Argentina 1. It ended la nuestra, the aesthetic creed that had ruled for three decades, and opened the age of anti-fútbol.

La Máquina and la nuestra

Isolation, brought about by war and Perónist foreign policy, meant no foreign defeats ever forced a rethink, and Argentinian football went ever further down the road of aestheticism. Its shrine was Renato Cesarini's River Plate of the early forties: a metodo shape with Néstor Rossi, the 'Howler of the Americas', as a forward-thinking centre-half behind the fabled front five of Muñoz, Moreno, Pedernera, Labruna and Loustau.

Moreno and Pedernera dropped into the space in front of the half-line. Loustau patrolled the whole left flank as the ventilador-wing, giving air to the midfield by doing some of its running. That let the right-half Norberto Yácono, 'The Stamp', stick to a man and become, in effect, a third back.

They called us the 'Knights of Anguish' because we didn't look for the goal. In the midfield we had fun. There was no rush. — Juan Carlos Muñoz
River Plate, c. 1941–45

The Angels with Dirty Faces, 1957

Copa América, Peru. Corbatta, Maschio, Angelillo, Sívori and Cruz scored eight against Colombia, three against Ecuador, four against Uruguay, six against Chile and three against Brazil, with a mischievous verve that earned them their nickname. Argentina's isolation was emphatically over: they were the best side in South America, and possibly the world.

By Sweden 1958, Maschio, Angelillo and Sívori had been sold to Serie A (all three would end up playing for Italy), Di Stéfano had thrown in his lot with Spain, and cover was so thin that Ángel Labruna was recalled as he approached forty.

Helsingborg, 15 June 1958

A 3–1 defeat to West Germany, then a 3–1 win over Northern Ireland complete with party-pieces ('a lot of little fat men with stomachs, smiling at us and waving at girls in the crowd,' said Jimmy McIlroy), and a draw needed against Czechoslovakia. Milan Dvořák scored after eight minutes; Zdeněk Zikán added two before half-time; Corbatta pulled one back from the spot; Feureisl and two late goals from Hovorka made it 6–1.

The Czechs scored four goals past me that were identical. They pulled a cross back and it was a goal. They pulled another cross back and it was another goal. — Amadeo Carrizo

'We were used to playing really slowly, and they were fast,' said José Ramos Delgado. The squad was pelted with coins and vegetables at Ezeiza and Guillermo Stábile, coach since 1941, was dismissed. 'He was a romantic.'

The metodo was obsolete and Argentina switched to 4-2-4, but with its own arithmetic. Because the right-half, the 4, had long since dropped in as a third back, it was the left-half, the 6, who now fell into central defence beside the 2, with the 3 to his left, while the centre-half, the 5, stayed on as a holding midfielder. An English back four reads 2-5-6-3; an Argentinian one 4-2-6-3. Positions are still known by number: Rattín was a '5', Ardiles an '8'.

At Boca, Vicente Feola's Brazilian import Orlando 'introduced us to the idea of a caged number 6, sitting in defence rather than in midfield,' said Rattín.

Sacrifice over play: Boca, 1962–64

'A different kind of player was looked for, players who were more about sacrifice than play,' said Ramos Delgado. 'Football became less of an art after that.' Boca's 1962 title came with a 4-3-3 in which Alberto González tracked back as a ventilador — a tornante, the Italians would say — to become a fourth midfielder. Two years later, under Adolfo Pedernera of la Máquina, they won again conceding fifteen goals in thirty games and scoring thirty-five.

The Bohemian from before doesn't exist any more. Today the message is clear: if you win, you are useful, if you lose, you are not. — Pedernera
Boca Juniors

Stop the food leaving the kitchen

The philosopher Tomás Abraham calls it a 'first modernity': European discipline, physical training, hygiene, Fordism. It split Argentinian football between those who wanted to keep the tradition and those who insisted the country had been left behind — most famously, later, Carlos Bilardo against César Luis Menotti, but first Ángel Labruna against Juan Carlos Lorenzo. Lorenzo, appointed for the 1962 World Cup explicitly to bring a European approach, tried to install catenaccio, with the libero in a different-coloured shirt in training, before running out of time and reverting to 4-2-4.

If you don't want somebody to eat, you have to stop the food coming out of the kitchen. I don't send somebody to mark the waiter; I have to worry about the chef. — Lorenzo

Wembley, 1966: the diamond and Rattín

Reappointed for 1966, Lorenzo introduced what became the classic Argentinian shape, the 4-3-1-2: Antonio Rattín at the base of a midfield diamond, Jorge Solari and Alberto González shuttling either side of him as carrileros, Ermindo Onega at the point, and width from the surging full-backs Roberto Ferreiro and Silvio Marzolini. England's shape was much the same, except that where they had a designated destroyer in Nobby Stiles, Argentina had a designated creator in Onega.

Put the refereeing conspiracies to one side and both countries agree on why England won the quarter-final: Stiles silenced Onega, and Alan Ball, attacking from the right of midfield, stopped Marzolini getting forward. Rattín, the 5 and the captain, was sent off in the first half of a game the two nations still cannot agree about.

Racing v Celtic, 1967

The bigger change after 1958 was less the system than the style: Argentinian football became increasingly violent. Celtic won the first leg of the Intercontinental Cup 1–0 in Glasgow and walked into a storm in Buenos Aires: Ronnie Simpson felled by a stone in the warm-up, Norberto Raffo's header from what Celtic swore was offside, Juan Carlos Cárdenas's winner, and no water in the dressing room at half-time. A year on from the World Cup quarter-final there was a score to settle, and the distinctions between the parts of Great Britain meant little.

For the Montevideo play-off Jock Stein declared 'the time for politeness is over'. Cárdenas scored again; Celtic had four men sent off and Racing two. Celtic fined their players. Racing bought theirs new cars.

Zubeldía's laboratory: the offside trap

Osvaldo Zubeldía, schooled in Victorio Spinetto's garra at Vélez, arrived at Estudiantes de La Plata in 1965, kept four senior players, and introduced a pre-season, concentración the night before games and tactics on a blackboard. His 4-3-3 did two things no side in South America did: it pressed, and it played an aggressive offside trap, probably lifted from a video of Maslov's Dynamo Kyiv.

It was something unknown in Argentina, and it was that that allowed a humble team like Estudiantes to have such success. — Juan Ramón Verón

They won the 1967 Metropolitano, the first title for a club from outside the capital in thirty-six years, then three straight Libertadores.

The rehearsed corner

All the possibilities afforded by the game were foreseen and practised. Corners, free-kicks, throw-ins were used to our best advantage and we also had secret signs and language which we used to make our opponents fall into the trap. — Carlos Bilardo

Buenos Aires, 1968, Intercontinental Cup first leg. Amid the hair-pulling, the punch in George Best's stomach and the head-butt that cut Nobby Stiles's eye, Marcos Conigliaro headed in a Verón corner. A 1–1 draw at Old Trafford — Verón again, Willie Morgan's late equaliser — made Estudiantes world champions. Paddy Crerand called them 'the dirtiest team I've played against'; the Daily Mirror ran 'The night they spat on sportsmanship'.

La Bombonera, 1969

Anti-fútbol — the term was coined during the 1968 Libertadores — went too far in September 1969. Estudiantes lost the first leg 3–0 in Milan and won the return 2–1, but Aguirre Suárez broke Néstor Combin's cheekbone with an elbow, the goalkeeper Alberto Poletti punched Gianni Rivera and Eduardo Manera kicked him to the ground. Onganía spoke of shame and the revulsion of a nation; all three were sentenced to thirty days in jail for disgracing a public spectacle.

Zubeldía himself had walked on to the pitch to try to stop it, but the stain would not wash out — even if, as Walter Vargas noted, Pizzuti's Racing, Independiente and Rattín's Boca 'were not exactly a monastery of Trappist monks'.

The argument begins

Defeats in Bolivia and Peru kept Argentina out of the 1970 World Cup; Chacarita Juniors beat River 4–1 in the Metropolitano final by actually playing football; and El Gráfico turned: 'The desire to erase the memory of those six Czechoslovakian goals propelled us towards a more defensive game, towards the eternal fear of losing.'

Spinetto begat Zubeldía, and Zubeldía begat Bilardo, whose name became synonymous with the whole philosophy of anti-fútbol. Against it stood the memory of la Máquina and the tradition of la nuestra, the side that would find its voice in Menotti. Argentina would argue about it for the next fifty years.
