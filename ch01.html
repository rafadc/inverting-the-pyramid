Chapter 1

From Genesis to the Pyramid

Football began as chaos and was given form by Victorians who mistrusted anything cleverer than a head-down charge. This chapter follows the game from the medieval mob and the public-school dribblers to the afternoon in 1872 when Queen’s Park passed the ball around a heavier England, and on to the 2-3-5 Pyramid that Britain would keep for thirty-five years.

Chaos: the mob game

Two teams, a roughly spherical object and a target at each end of a notional pitch. The medieval mob game was violent, unruly and anarchic, and it was repeatedly outlawed. Even after the Victorian public schools tamed it into something recognisable, formations were unheard of: teams simply chased the ball, and for years the number of players and the length of a match were not fixed. If there was a shape at all, it amounted to two or three backs and eight or nine forwards.

The dribbling game and backing up

In the cloisters of Charterhouse and Westminster a mob would have broken bones, so the dribbling game grew up there instead. A prefect ran with the ball at his feet while his team-mates lined up behind him, backing up in case it bounced loose in a tackle. The FA’s original Law Six of 1863 put anyone ahead of the kicker out of play, so a pass could only go sideways or backwards. For Englishmen, charging head-down at goal was the manly thing; passing, cooperation and defending looked suspiciously subtle. Even a decade later Charles Alcock felt he had to explain the principle of backing up.

‘a sight not to be forgotten’ — an unnamed writer of the 1870s, on a good dribble

Partick, 1872: the first international

Football’s first international was played at the West of Scotland cricket ground in Partick in 1872. England named a goal, a three-quarter back, a half-back, a fly-kick, four middles, two on the left side and one on the right: in modern notation, a lop-sided 1-2-7, with seven forwards and only four men in the three lines of defence. Scotland were represented entirely by the Queen’s Park club, more than a stone a man lighter, and set out in a definite 2-2-6. Almost everyone expected English weight and pace to tell.

England 1-2-7 Scotland 2-2-6

Passing the ball around England

Rather than be out-muscled man for man, Queen’s Park passed the ball around their opponents. The heavier, faster English forwards chased it and kept arriving where it had just been. The game finished 0–0, a shock for a side with a far larger pool of players and a longer tradition. Passing had been part of the Scottish game from the start: Queen’s Park’s own offside rule of 1867 was far more permissive, and their isolation, with only three real fixtures in 1871–72, meant the style was hot-housed in practice matches among themselves. From it grew the pattern-weaving game, strings of short passes zigzagging between the half- and forward-lines.

‘they played excellently well together’ — the Glasgow Herald on Scotland

1-1-8, 1-2-7, 2-2-6

Notation is a modern imposition on these sides, but the drift is clear. The earliest teams sent almost everyone forward behind a lone back or two. England’s 1872 shape added a second half-back; Scotland’s 2-2-6 gave up a forward to have two full-backs. Even that much defending offended the purists. In 1882 the Scottish Athletic Journal mocked country clubs that kept two men twenty yards from their own goal, and Lugar Boswell Thistle of Ayrshire were deplored for attacking with a mere nine men.

‘to keep the goalkeeper in chat’ — the Scottish Athletic Journal on the two men back

The Pyramid: 2-3-5

In a passing game the two centre-forwards found themselves duplicating each other, so over the course of the 1880s one of them slipped back into a deeper position and became a centre-half. Two, three, five: the Pyramid. Cambridge University are usually credited with it in 1883, though there is evidence they used it six years earlier. Nottingham Forest were advocates by the late 1870s, inspired by their captain Sam Widdowson, who also invented the shinpad, and Wrexham withdrew E.A. Cross from the forward line to win the 1878 Welsh Cup final. Dumbarton won the 1883 Scottish Cup final with the shape, and England first used it against Scotland in 1884.

The withdrawn centre-forward

The centre-half as fulcrum

The new centre-half quickly became the fulcrum of the team, a figure far removed from the dour stopper he would later become. He was a multi-skilled all-rounder: defender and attacker, leader and instigator, goal-scorer and destroyer. Everything went through him and everything came back. Two decades on, when instructional columns by professionals began to appear in the Edwardian press, it was Woolwich Arsenal’s centre-half Percy Sands who asked whether football was becoming more scientific.

‘the most important man on the field’ — Willy Meisl

The Scottish professors

Passing travelled south with people. Henry Renny-Tailyour and John Blackburn, army lieutenants who had beaten England for Scotland, carried the combination style to the Royal Engineers, and the Reverend Spencer Walker gave every Lancing forward a fixed place. Preston North End, who had lined up in a 2-2-6 in 1879, were transformed by a host of Scottish players, professionals in all but name; by 1883 their team-sheets showed a 2-3-5, and a Glasgow teacher, James Gledhill, lectured them by blackboard on what a team of experts might do. Preston won the first two Football League titles, the first without losing a game.

‘The forwards tip-tap the ball here and there’ — Frank Buckley

Two ways to pass

Short passing against long, science against physique: that contrast underlay almost every debate about British football up to the First World War. Blackburn Olympic, a side of weavers and spinners, took the FA Cup north for the first time in 1883 with sweeping passes from wing to wing. West Bromwich Albion, a local Staffordshire side on ten pounds a week, beat Preston’s expensive Scottish artists in the 1888 final with the open game: long balls from the centre to the wings and from one inside man to the opposite flanker. In Scotland there was no doubt which style was purer, and the Dumbartonshire long-ball clubs were sneered at by the Glasgow press.

‘the Scottish game is actually slower’ — Looker-On, 1910

Thirty-five years of the Pyramid

Scotland adopted the centre-half in 1887, grumbling about copying an English idea; by 1889 the debate was over. For the next three and a half decades the 2-3-5 was Britain’s default, and variations were left to the captain rather than to a coach. Tom Boyle of Burnley and Barnsley would direct play towards whichever flank looked weakest, and Barnsley’s trainer once laid out twenty-two sugar lumps on a table at Lytham to plan a cup final. Real change would have to wait for a rewritten offside law, and for the coffee houses of Vienna and the streets of Buenos Aires, where the next chapter begins.
