Chapter 16

The Return of the Back Three

Italy's libero game was undone by Hamburg in 1983, and out of its wreckage — from Buenos Aires, Zagreb and Copenhagen at once — came a back three with wing-backs. Bilardo's 3-5-2 won a World Cup with Maradona free behind the strikers; by 1990 everybody had one, and the goals dried up. Step through the spare-man arithmetic and how wing-backs turn a 3-5-2 into a 5-3-2.

The schism closes

After Brazil's 4-2-4 exposed the W-M in 1958, Europe split in two. The north — Britain, the Netherlands, later Scandinavia — answered with a back four and pressing; the south — Italy and the Balkans — with a libero. Germany sat awkwardly in between, sweeping but not pressing until the mid-nineties. Wilson toys with the idea that the line follows the Protestant–Catholic divide: the work ethic of constant movement against the patience of defending deep. By the nineties both schools had drifted toward the same answer, a back three with wing-backs. Watch one team pass through both traditions and arrive where they met.

One team, three shapes: 4-4-2, then 1-3-3-3, then 3-5-2

Il gioco all'Italiana: everyone marks someone

By the early eighties every Italian side played the same system, and it showed in the shirt numbers. The 9 was the centre-forward, the 11 attacked from the left, the 7 was the tornante on the right; the 2 marked the 11, the 5 marked the 9, the 6 swept behind. Because everyone was equally asymmetric, the marking always lined up. In the 1983 European Cup final Juventus met Hamburg's two forwards: Gentile took Lars Bastrup, Brio took Horst Hrubesch, Scirea swept, and Cabrini, with nobody to mark, was free to attack down the left.

Juventus Hamburg

Happel's switch

Ernst Happel simply moved Bastrup to the other flank, onto Cabrini. Nobody did that in Italy. Trapattoni kept faith with man-marking and sent Gentile across to follow him, which left a hole on Juventus's right that Marco Tardelli was supposed to drop into. He neither covered the space nor attacked, and Felix Magath ran through the gap to score the only goal. A system that had beaten Brazil less than a year earlier was undone by a single idea: switch the marker's man and the whole chain slips.

The seed of the 3-5-2

Wilson's point is that the Italian game already contained its successor. Pull the tornante back into midfield, shuffle the marking right-back a little toward the centre and the stopper a touch to the left, then advance the left-back, and what remains is a 3-5-2: a compact back three, two men supplying the width, and eight bodies down the middle. It suited a culture that had distrusted wingers since Brera, and it is where the strand of Italian football that never followed Sacchi ended up. Bilardo's arithmetic: seven and three

Carlos Bilardo reached the same shape from a different problem: how to fit a playmaker into a coherent defence. His answer was a split of the outfield ten, and if there were no wingers left in the game, why keep full-backs? Redesignate them as midfielders and push them up. Against England in 1986 he finally settled his eleven: Brown sweeping behind the markers Ruggeri and Cuciuffo, Batista in front, Giusti and Olarticoechea wide, Enrique added for control, Burruchaga and Valdano ahead — and Maradona free to roam wherever he pleased.

Wing-backs: push on, drop back

Ćiro Blažević insists he invented the 3-5-2 at Dinamo Zagreb in 1982, and whatever the truth, his version shows the shape's real trick. Velimir Zajec swept behind Hadžić and Bogdan, always with two men covering when he carried the ball out. The full-backs, Brnčić and Bošnjak, were pushed high as midfielders, and opponents unused to the shape often went a goal or two down inside twenty minutes. Once ahead, Dinamo dropped them back into a 5-3-2 to check the wingers. Same eleven, two shapes: the wing-backs decide which one you are watching.

Piontek's spare man

Sepp Piontek's Denmark reached the back three from the Dutch 1-3-3-3, and he explained the logic plainly. Opponents attacked with one or two forwards, so a back four left two men idle; three would do, with Morten Olsen the spare man behind the markers Busk and Nielsen. The player saved became a midfielder, where the real work was, and if trouble came Bertelsen could drop in again. It was efficient and it was fun: Arnesen and Jesper Olsen were genuine attacking wide men, Elkjær and Laudrup a roving front two. Fifa called it the most spectacular football of 1986.

What wingers do to it

The system's weakness was just as plain. In the quarter-final, two goals down to Maradona, Bobby Robson sent on John Barnes and Chris Waddle — real wingers. Argentina's three centre-backs were all in the middle, so each wing-back was suddenly alone against a dribbler with room to run into. Barnes beat Giusti again and again, Lineker converted one cross and came within inches of another in the final seconds. Belgium, in the semi-final, had no wide players to ask the same question. A back three assumes the opponent will not use the flanks; the moment they do, the arithmetic changes.

The final: narrowness, then a pass beyond Briegel

Beckenbauer's West Germany met Argentina with their own back three and gave Matthäus the job of marking Maradona. Maradona simply stood high on the German line and pinned him there, so it looked as if Germany had four centre-backs behind two holders, with Magath cut off and the full-backs not allowed to push on. Argentina led 2–0. Only when Matthäus was released did Germany come alive, scoring twice from corners and, for once, leaving space behind. Three minutes later Maradona slipped a pass beyond Briegel and Burruchaga ran on to win the World Cup.

Italia 90: everybody's back three

By 1990 Bilardo's thinking looked like common sense. West Germany won with Augenthaler, Buchwald and Kohler behind Matthäus, Reuter and Brehme as attacking full-backs, and could change tone without changing shape — Buchwald stepped into midfield to break up the Dutch. Even England found a libero, Mark Wright between Butcher and Walker, and let Waddle, Platt and Gascoigne loose. But goals fell to a record 2.21 a game and red cards climbed. The shape rewarded muscle and swapped the winger for the wing-back; its other legacy was to push the playmaker up into a second striker.
