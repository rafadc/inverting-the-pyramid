Chapter 12

Total Football

On opposite sides of Europe, Rinus Michels in Amsterdam and Valeriy Lobanovskyi in Kyiv arrived at the same idea at the same time: football is a contest for space. Make the pitch big when you have the ball and small when you do not, and let any player take any position. What made it possible was pressing, and pressing needed full-time professionals fit enough to run for ninety minutes.

Never had so much room

Dutch football was a backwater in the fifties. Between 1949 and 1955 the national side won two of twenty-seven matches and lost twice to Norway. When England won 8–2 at Huddersfield in 1948 the Dutch still lined up in the old 2-3-5, long after the rest of Europe had moved to the W-M, and Tommy Lawton scored four times in the gap between two backs and three half-backs.

"Never had so much room." — Tommy Lawton

Limited professionalism arrived only in 1954. Wilson's point is that skipping the W-M era was a hidden gift: rigid man-to-man marking never took root, so there was nothing to unlearn.

Netherlands 2-3-5   England W-M, Lawton at centre-forward

Two English teachers

Ajax's founding father was Jack Reynolds, a journeyman English professional who arrived in 1915 and spent twenty-five years at the club over three spells, interrupted by a boardroom row and by wartime internment alongside P.G. Wodehouse. He trained with the ball, built a youth system in which every age group played the same way, and treated attack as the best defence; the club's own couplet warned against neglecting the wing.

Vic Buckingham, arriving in 1959 with Tottenham's push-and-run inheritance, added the possession creed. His Ajax won the 1960 title averaging 3.2 goals a game, two players carrying the ball thirty yards down the left with short passes and cutting out three defenders.

"If you've got the ball, keep it. The other side can't score."
Ajax, from Reynolds's 2-3-5 to Buckingham's fluid W-M

Michels, the animal trainer

Buckingham was sacked in January 1965 with Ajax near the bottom, and the club turned to Rinus Michels, an easy-going former centre-forward who had studied at the Amsterdam sports academy and taught gymnastics. As a coach he was transformed into an obsessive disciplinarian, hard even on his assistants.

"The main thing with him was now discipline. Fantastic discipline."

He kept Ajax up, won the title the next season and made the whole squad fully professional so that training could be as demanding as he liked. Tactically he junked the W-M for the 4-2-4 then sweeping Europe: Keizer, Cruyff, Swart and Groot in attack, the combative Bennie Muller beside the more technical Klaas Nuninga. There was no grand plan yet.

Ajax 1965–66, W-M into 4-2-4

Vasović, Happel and the third midfielder

Liverpool were thrashed 5–1 at De Meer in 1966, but elimination by Dukla Prague showed Michels's ruthlessness: Tonny Pronk moved into midfield and the captain Frits Soetekouw was sold, replaced by Velibor Vasović, a Yugoslav sweeper who dropped behind the back four and then stepped out as an extra midfielder, a habit Dutch football never lost.

"I was the architect, together with Michels, of the aggressive way of defending."

Ajax won four titles in five years but were still overrun in midfield. In spring 1970 Bertie Mee called them amateurish and a 3–3 draw with Feyenoord settled it. Ernst Happel's Rotterdam side, Rinus Israël sweeping, Wim van Hanegem creating between Hasil and Jansen, Coen Moulijn on the left, were already in 4-3-3 and on their way to the European Cup.

Ajax 4-2-4 with Vasović as libero   Feyenoord 4-3-3, 1970

Pressing, and offside as attack

Pressing began with Johan Neeskens, sent to hound the opposing playmaker and chasing him deep into enemy territory. Team-mates learned to move up behind him, which meant a very high line, and Vasović timed his step forward to leave opposing forwards stranded offside. Michels wanted more than a free kick: with four or five opponents out of the game, the spare Ajax defenders were to charge the man on the ball and turn the trap into an attack.

"That's how offside becomes an offensive game."

The space behind the defence stopped mattering, because nobody was given time to find it. Ajax could press for an hour, Swart claimed; helped, Hulshoff later admitted, by the club doctor's pills.

Ajax 1970–71   Opponent in possession

Make the pitch big, make it small

Wilson opens the chapter with the idea Michels and Lobanovskyi reached independently: football is a matter of space, and the side that controls it wins. With the ball the Dutch stretched the pitch, full-backs wide and wingers on the touchline, so possession was easy to keep. Without it they compressed into a narrow band, so the opponent had nowhere to play.

"It was all about space."

Marinho Peres, who faced them with Brazil in 1974 and then joined Michels at Barcelona, learned that if the squeeze produced no chance the defenders simply dropped off and made the pitch big again. David Winner links this feel for space to a flat, flooded, endlessly re-planned country.

Any player, any position

Interchanging positions became the trademark, but it grew out of necessity. Opponents packed their defences against Ajax, and Celtic's 1967 European Cup had shown that massed attacks beat massed defences, so defenders had to join in. The 4-3-3 made the rotations easy to organise because they ran down channels: Suurbier, Haan and Swart on the right; Vasović, Neeskens and Cruyff through the centre; Krol, Mühren and Keizer on the left.

"You make space, you come into space." — Barry Hulshoff

The novelty was that the swaps were longitudinal, defender to midfielder to forward, rather than the lateral shuffles of Arkadiev's Dinamo Moscow. Michels found the hard part was not persuading a full-back to attack but finding someone to cover for him.

The three rotating triangles; Hulshoff stays home as cover

Kovács and three European Cups

Michels won the first European Cup in 1971 and left for Barcelona. Ajax replaced him with the cheapest name on a list of fifteen, the Romanian Ștefan Kovács, who bought a return ticket just in case. He swapped Vasović for Horst Blankenburg and loosened the reins: Blankenburg, Suurbier and Krol attacked freely because Neeskens, Haan and Mühren dropped in behind them.

"I've been employed as a coach, not a hairdresser."

In 1971–72 Ajax were at their most fluent, beating Internazionale 2–0 in the final with two Cruyff goals. In 1973 Gerrie Mühren juggled the ball in the Bernabéu and Juventus were beaten 1–0 in Belgrade, a fourth-minute goal followed by long, taunting strings of passes: three European Cups in a row, the first since Real Madrid.

Ajax 1972: full-backs and libero advance while the midfield drops to cover

Oranje, 1974

The word totaalvoetbal was only coined when the national team took Ajax's game to the 1974 World Cup, in orange, under Michels, with Feyenoord's Jansen and Van Hanegem in midfield. The champions Brazil were beaten 2–0 in Dortmund. Their captain Marinho Peres later joined Barcelona and struggled with what Brazilians mocked as the donkey line, a flat defence that stepped up together; at home cover was sacred and beating one defender never meant beating them all.

"The whole logic of the offside trap comes from squeezing the game."

Cruyff explained it to him: skilful Brazilians or Argentinians could not be given a huge pitch, so everybody was squeezed into a thin band and nobody had time to chip a pass over the top.

The final, and the fall

In Munich the Dutch scored in the first minute and then tried what Ajax had done to Juventus, holding the ball in long strings of passes. West Germany won 2–1. Cruyff, wearing two stripes rather than Adidas's three out of loyalty to Puma, was already a Barcelona player: when George Knobel let the Ajax squad vote on the captaincy for 1973–74 they chose Piet Keizer, and Cruyff left after two more games, Neeskens following a year later.

"Ajax was his opera." — Florin Halagian, on Kovács

Knobel was sacked in 1974 after accusing his players of drinking and womanising. The freedom that produced the peak hastened the collapse; Total Football lived on at Barcelona under Michels and, as the next chapter shows, in Kyiv.
