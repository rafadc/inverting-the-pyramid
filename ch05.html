Chapter 5

Organised Disorder

Football came late to the USSR and, cut off from Fifa, it stayed in the 2-3-5 long after the rest of Europe had moved on. In 1937 a touring Basque side arrived playing the W-M and won almost everything. What Boris Arkadiev built from the shock — a W-M whose forwards refused to stand where the programme said they would — beguiled Britain in 1945 and pointed the way to 4-2-4.

A late boom, an old shape

British sailors played by the docks in Odessa in the 1860s. Harry Charnock, manager of the Morozov Mills, founded the club that became Dinamo Moscow to keep his workers off the vodka on Saturdays, and dressed them in the blue and white of the team he supported: Blackburn Rovers.

By the time a national league arrived in 1936 the British were long gone, but the 2-3-5 lingered as the default. The 1925 offside change made little difference, and with the USSR isolated from Fifa nothing exposed how far behind the Soviets were falling.

Soviet 2-3-5: two backs, three halves, five forwards

1937: the Basques bring the W-M

A Basque side touring the world to raise awareness of their cause during the Spanish Civil War — six of them from Spain's 1934 World Cup squad — arrived playing a W-M. They hammered Lokomotiv 5–1, beat Dinamo 2–1, drew 2–2 with a Leningrad XI and put seven past a Dinamo Select XI.

Against two full-backs, the centre-forward Isidro Lángara had all the room he wanted.

Basque W-M   Soviet 2-3-5

Spartak's third back

Nikolai Starostin, head of Spartak's coaching council, called up players from other clubs and matched the Basques shape for shape, converting his centre-half — his brother Andrei — into a third back to shackle Lángara. Andrei was not pleased.

"Do you want me to be famous across the whole Soviet Union? You are denying me room to breathe! Who will help the attack?"

Party officials slept at the Tarasovka training base. Spartak won 6–2, Vladimir Stepanov scoring a hat-trick, though the goalkeeper and the newspapers noted that Lángara had still beaten Andrei in the air and scored.

Spartak   Basques

Pravda's lesson

The defeat was an aberration. The Basques went on to beat Dynamo Kyiv, Dinamo Tbilisi, a Georgia XI and finally a Minsk XI 6–1. Under the headline "Soviet Players should become Invincible", Pravda conceded that the best Soviet teams were far from high quality and listed what the visitors had taught: long passes, playing on the flanks, heading the ball.

The W-M, it had been recognised, offered a number of intriguing possibilities.

Arkadiev, the fencing master

Boris Arkadiev, born in St Petersburg in 1899, taught fencing at the Frunze Military Academy while he played. Parry and riposte, he said, convinced him of the value of counter-attacking. He took Metallurg to third in the first league season, then took over champions Dinamo and won the double.

But as every leading Soviet team adopted the W-M after the Basque tour, Dinamo slid to fifth in 1938 and ninth in 1939. With Lavrenty Beria as the club's patron, something drastic was required.

Dinamo Moscow   Opposition

Gagry, February 1940: the roaming forward

At a pre-season camp on the Black Sea, Arkadiev spent an unprecedented two-hour session teaching nothing but tactics. His refinement of the W-M: four of the five forwards hold orthodox positions, moving to and fro in their channels — and one suddenly disrupts the pattern, running diagonally or from left to right.

"That made it difficult for the defending team to follow him, and the other forwards benefited because they had a free team-mate to whom they could pass."

Organised disorder

After a poor start, Arkadiev sat every player down and made him write a report on himself and his team-mates. Then it clicked: Dynamo Kyiv beaten 8–5 and 7–0, champions Spartak 5–1, and seven straight wins to finish the season with twenty-six scored and three conceded.

Left-winger Sergei Ilyin scored most of his goals from centre-forward, right-winger Mikhail Semichastny from inside-left, centre-forward Sergei Soloviov from the flanks. The newspapers called it "organised disorder"; Arkadiev called it breathing the Russian soul into the English invention.

Dinamo's five forwards step round each other's positions

The man-marking answer

Opponents replied with strict man-to-man marking. Arkadiev's response was to interchange even more: all the attackers and even the midfielders roaming, while his own defenders switched to a mobile system, following their opponents wherever they went.

A marker who follows his man gets dragged wherever the man goes. The space he leaves behind is the point.

Dinamo   Man-markers tethered to their men

Towards 4-2-4

In England the W-M's defensive logic — every player with a designated opponent — had grown organically. In the USSR the system arrived fully formed, and the consequences had to be worked out in a hurry.

Gradually one half dropped in to cover in front of the back three, which meant an inside-forward dropping to cover him. 3-2-2-3 was on its way to becoming 4-2-4; the historian Axel Vartanyan believes Arkadiev was probably the first man to deploy a flat back four.

1945: passovotchka at Stamford Bridge

Arkadiev left for CDKA in 1943, but Dinamo kept his principles and brought their short-passing game — passovotchka, the British called it — on a goodwill tour after the war. Chelsea scrambled a fortunate 3–3 draw. As Sindelar had, as Nándor Hidegkuti would, Konstantin Beskov bewildered them by refusing to stand where a centre-forward was supposed to stand.

"The Russians were on the move all the time. We could hardly keep up with them." — Albert Tennant, Chelsea left-back
Dinamo   Chelsea

A Chinese puzzle

Davie Meiklejohn, the former Rangers captain, watched them interchange positions "to the extent of the outside-left running over to the right-wing and vice versa". Trying to match the players to the programme was a Chinese puzzle, he wrote — yet the most remarkable feature of all was that they never got in each other's way.

Cardiff were thrashed 10–1, Arsenal beaten 4–3, Rangers held 2–2. Geoffrey Simpson in the Daily Mail judged their football "way ahead of our own".

Cogs or individuals?

Was it ideology? Alex James, the old Arsenal inside-forward, saw teamwork to which there was a pattern, no individualist like a Matthews or a Carter — and called that lack a great weakness. Mikhail Yakushin, now Dinamo's coach, was happy to agree: collective football first, individual football second, and no place for a Matthews.

Or perhaps Beskov, Bobrov and Kartsev, fine technical players all, simply used their gifts in a different way.

The pattern   The individualist

Blessed with wingers

Britain ignored the lesson. Bob McGory tried passovotchka at Stoke — with Stanley Matthews in his side — and failed. Why change a shape that let Matthews, Tom Finney and Jimmy Mullen in England, Willie Waddell, Jimmy Delaney and Gordon Smith in Scotland, run free down the touchline?

Matthews' finest hour came in the 1953 FA Cup final; six months later, on the same pitch, Hungary won 6–3. Herbert Chapman had built the W-M to circumvent wingers, and the wingers ended up setting it in stone.
