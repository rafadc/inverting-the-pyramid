Chapter 10

Catenaccio

The word means ‘chain’, as in the chain on a door. To generations it summoned up Italian football at its most paranoid and brutal – so reviled that when Celtic beat Herrera’s Inter in Lisbon in 1967, Bill Shankly told Jock Stein the win had made him ‘immortal’. Yet the bolt’s origins were homely: a café owner in Geneva, a sleepless coach on a fishing quay, a butcher’s grandson from Trieste.

Eleven average footballers

Karl Rappan was born in Vienna in 1905, a forward of the coffee-house golden age who would later run the Café de la Bourse in Geneva. When he became player-coach at Servette in 1930 his men were semi-professionals who could not match full-time sides for fitness, so he set out to beat class with a plan. The shape he started from was the one Vienna still swore by: the 2-3-5.

‘Either you have eleven individuals who, owing to sheer class, are enabled to beat their opponents – Brazil would be an example – or you have eleven average footballers who have to be integrated into a plan.’
Servette

The verrou

A Swiss journalist named it the verrou – the bolt. Where Chapman’s W-M dropped the centre-half between the full-backs, Rappan dropped both wing-halves back to flank them and take the wingers. The two full-backs became, in effect, central defenders, side by side.

Watch the bolt slide. When the attack comes down one flank the near defender steps to the ball and the far one slips in behind him. Whichever way play goes there is always a spare man at the back: the verrouilleur, the man who would become the libero.

Servette   Opponents in a W-M

A spare man here, a man short there

The three forwards stayed high as pure attackers, so against a W-M the lone centre-half was left with two inside-forwards; against a 2-3-5 he had their centre-half to cope with as well. Rappan’s remedy was to drop deep, cede the midfield and mark so tightly that opponents ended up passing sideways in frustration. In time an inside-forward fell back beside the centre-half, and one full-back dropped behind the other as an orthodox sweeper.

Switzerland, whose Gerö Cup record read played 32, won 4, beat England 2–1 and knocked out Germany at the 1938 World Cup. On the Volga, Krylya Sovetov’s gymnast-coach Alexander Abramov reached the same idea from the W-M – the ‘Volga Clip’ – and Soviet critics came to call it ‘the right of the weak’.

The reserve net

Gipo Viani’s story: a sleepless dawn walk along Salerno’s waterfront, and a fishing boat hauling in one net swollen with fish and then, behind it, a second to catch whatever slipped through. Over-romanticised, perhaps, but his Salernitana of 1946–47 were the first to use the system regularly. Half-back Alberto Piccinini dropped in to mark the centre-forward, and the middle of the three W-M defenders fell back behind the line as a sweeper.

Sit deep, draw them on, catch them on the counter – Chapman’s thinking at Northampton in a different shape. The vianema won promotion with the best defensive record in the second tier; in Serie A they failed to win away and went straight back down.

The tornante and the free hitter

Catenaccio remained a small club’s tactic until Alfredo Foni’s Internazionale won the 1952–53 scudetto with it. Gino Armano, the right-winger, dropped back to mark the opposing left-winger – the first tornante, or ‘returner’ – which let right-back Ivano Blason slide across behind the defence. Blason would scratch a line in the turf and dare forwards to cross it; the battitore libero, the ‘free hitter’, simply belted the ball clear or, in Gianni Brera’s phrase, fired a mortar shot seventy metres into empty space for Lorenzi, Skoglund and Nyers.

Inter scored 46 goals in 34 games, 27 fewer than Juventus, conceded 24, won eight matches 1–0 and took the title. Fiorentina followed in 1956 with left-half Armando Segato as libero and Prini as the tornante.

Rocco’s Milan

Nereo Rocco, a butcher’s grandson and Christian Democrat councillor from Trieste, took a Triestina side that had finished bottom to joint second in 1948, gave Padova the best years in its history, and in 1961 inherited AC Milan from Viani. His catenaccio was nothing like the stereotype: Milan won the 1961–62 title with 83 goals, 22 more than anyone else, with Cesare Maldini a ‘gentleman footballer’ at sweeper and Gianni Rivera – l’abatino, ‘the monk’, to the sceptical Brera – pulling the strings.

The rule was that everyone tracked back, forwards included; Jimmy Greaves scored nine in ten and left after five months. Twice in eight second-half minutes Rivera laid on goals for Altafini as Milan came from behind against Benfica in the 1963 European Cup final.

‘Those two need to understand that during a football match you get kicked, not just well paid.’
Milan   Benfica

‘I must have it!’

Helenio Herrera – born in Buenos Aires in 1910 or 1916, raised in Casablanca, capped by France at full-back – won two titles at Barcelona scoring 96 goals a season before Angelo Moratti, who had sacked twelve coaches in five years, paid him a record £35,000 a year. Il Mago controlled diet, sleep and psychology, confined his players to the ritiro at Appiano Gentile before games and rose before seven for yoga: ‘I am strong, I am calm, I fear nothing, I am beautiful.’ Before kick-off the players circled a ball and reached for it, chanting.

‘He who plays for himself plays for the opposition. He who plays for the team, plays for himself.’

‘When I started, managers carried the teams’ bags,’ he said. He was the first modern manager.

Inter

Take out a midfielder, free the left-back

Third, then second – and Moratti gave him one more season. Herrera changed shape: ‘I took out a midfielder and put him sweeping behind the main defenders, liberating the left-back to attack.’ Armando Picchi, one goal in his Serie A career, became the libero, ‘a defensive director’ whose passes ‘were never random’. Aristide Guarneri was the stopper, Tarcisio Burgnich sat alongside him and Carlo Tagnin, later Gianfranco Bedin, did the running in front.

Because the opposing right-winger was by now usually a tornante who sat deep, Giacinto Facchetti – signed as a forward – had licence to push on from left-back: ‘the first full-back to score as many goals as a forward’. Jair dropped in front of Burgnich, Corso was the link on the left, Mazzola the finisher.

Inter, 1962–67

Man-markers and the free man

Everyone had a man. Burgnich, Guarneri and Facchetti followed their forwards wherever they went; Tagnin took the inside-forward. Picchi belonged to nobody. He drifted behind the ball side, the reserve net waiting for whoever slipped the first, and when a marker was beaten the libero was already there.

Serie A in 1963, 1965 and 1966; European Cups in 1964 – Tagnin on Di Stéfano, Guarneri on Puskás, Real Madrid beaten 3–1 – and 1965.

Inter   Opposition forwards

Three passes to the box

‘Vertical football at great speed, with no more than three passes to get to the opponents’ box. If you lose the ball playing vertically, it’s not a problem – but lose it laterally and you pay with a goal.’

Picchi wins it. Suárez, the world’s most expensive player, hits it long from deep. Facchetti is already gone down the left – or Jair is running into space on the right – and Mazzola arrives. This was Herrera’s answer to the charge of negativity: those who copied him ‘forgot to include the attacking principles’.

The other side of la grande Inter was the skulduggery: referees in the club’s pay (Liverpool’s 3–1 Anfield lead overturned in 1965 with two goals Ortiz de Mendíbil should never have given), the ‘pharmacy cup coach’ rumours, the gamesmanship that so enraged Shankly.

Lisbon, 1967

Jock Stein’s Celtic played a 4-2-4 with a twist. Chalmers and Wallace took turns dropping deep to drag Inter’s markers out of position; Johnstone and Lennox drifted inside; and the full-backs, Craig and Gemmell, poured into the space they left. Mazzola’s seventh-minute penalty gave Inter a lead they knew how to protect – except that the belief had gone.

‘We just knew, even after fifteen minutes, that we were not going to keep them out.’ – Tarcisio Burgnich

Picchi had spent the night vomiting from tension. Seventeen minutes into the second half Murdoch found Craig, who cut the ball back for Gemmell to crash it into the top corner; five minutes from time Gemmell fed Murdoch and Chalmers diverted the mishit shot past Sarti. It was not possible to mark everybody, least of all those arriving from deep. Picchi turned to his keeper: ‘Giuliano, let it go.’

Celtic   Inter (Suárez injured)

Il gioco all’Italiana

A week later Inter lost the scudetto at Mantova; in 1968 they finished fifth and Herrera left for Roma. Rappan’s old problem – the swamped midfield – had never been solved, and the tornante eased it only by weakening the attack. Italy’s answer was to make the libero out of an inside-forward rather than a full-back: when his side had the ball he stepped up into midfield as an extra passer. Catenaccio became il gioco all’Italiana.

Catenaccio didn’t die with la grande Inter, but the myth of its invincibility did. Celtic had proved attacking football had a future, and it wasn’t just Shankly who was grateful.
