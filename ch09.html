Chapter 9

The Birth of the New

Old footage looks slow because nobody was closing anyone down. In sixties Kyiv a warm, bald, bushy-browed coach nicknamed Grandad changed that: Viktor Maslov withdrew his wingers to make the first 4-4-2, married zonal marking to a holding midfielder and taught his side to hunt the ball in packs. If modern football has a father, Wilson argues, it is Maslov.

Football in slow motion

Footage of Hungary in the fifties or Brazil in the sixties is startling for one reason: how much time every player has on the ball. Garrincha and Stanley Matthews needed three or four yards of acceleration before a feint could bite, and they got it every week because nobody closed them down. Wilson argues that the compression of that space, pressing, is what separates modern football from old. The idea is so simple that its slow, patchy spread is baffling: Germany adopted it only in the nineties, and Sacchi's Milan was hailed as revolutionary for doing what Ajax, Kyiv and even Watford had done years before.

Grandad comes to Kyiv

Viktor Maslov was an unlikely revolutionary: plump, bald and bushy-browed, known to his players as Grandad for his kindness rather than his years. A robust passing half for Torpedo Moscow in the thirties, he coached the club in four spells and won its first title in 1960. He arrived at Dynamo Kyiv in 1964, just as Soviet football, steered by national coach Gavriil Kachalin, was swapping the W-M for Brazil's 4-2-4. Maslov canvassed his senior players the night before each match and twice shrugged when a team waved away his own substitution. That trust bought him room for ideas that were, in their day, almost incomprehensible.

“Today you must be strong like lions, fast like stags, agile like panthers!”
Torpedo Moscow, then Dynamo Kyiv

Withdrawing the wingers

Like Alf Ramsey, Maslov saw how much of Brazil's 1958 success rested on Zagallo dropping off the left wing to become a third midfielder. Then he went one further and pulled his right-winger back as well. Ramsey is usually credited, or blamed, for abolishing wingers, and with almost no football traffic across the Iron Curtain he surely reached the idea on his own, but Wilson is clear that the 4-4-2 was Maslov's first. Crucially, the withdrawn men were converted forwards, Szabo, Serebryanykov and Biba among them, who kept a creative brief and worked as a second wave of attack rather than as a screen in front of the defence.

Dynamo Kyiv: 4-2-4 → 4-3-3 → 4-4-2

No place for wingers

A system built on withdrawn wingers had no use for a pure one, however gifted. Valeriy Lobanovskyi, nicknamed Cord because the ball seemed tied to his laces, was Dynamo's star; fans came to watch his backspun corners drop almost vertically into the box. In April 1964 he was substituted for the first time in his career, against Spartak, and his next match was his last for the club. Tales of a row over a vodka toast may be embroidered; the tactical logic was not. Kanevskyi and Bazylevych went the same way, and Lobanovskyi later admitted that as a player he could not have played in his own teams.

“Footballers who held on to the ball for too long didn't suit him.”

Full rights of democracy

Maslov was not against brilliance, only against individualism that ignored the collective. Andriy Biba, one of the finest midfielders Ukraine has produced, played for him as Bobby Charlton did for Ramsey: a playmaker with a free role, spared marking duties because everyone else defended zonally. In 1966 he beat Lev Yashin from forty yards, ran the 4-0 win over CSKA, scored the goal that won the cup final and the double, and was voted Soviet Player of the Year. Soviet coaches were obsessed with finding a Didí of their own; Maslov was the one who worked out how to build a team around him.

“Only Biba retains full rights of democracy.”
Dynamo Kyiv   Dinamo Moscow

Zonal marking

Biba's freedom rested on the forgotten innovation Zezé Moreira gave Brazil in 1954: zonal marking. Instead of shadowing one opponent, each defender took whoever entered his area and handed him on at its edge, which demands organisation and trust rather than improvisation. Nikolai Morozov tried it with the USSR before the 1966 World Cup, shipped six goals in two friendlies, panicked and fielded five defenders behind a sweeper. Maslov never wavered; for him the matter was almost ethical. By the end of 1967 even Martin Merzhanov, the doyen of Moscow's football writers, conceded that zonal defence had proved far more efficient than man-marking.

“Man-marking humiliates, insults and even morally oppresses the players who resort to it.”
Dynamo back line   Opposing forwards

Breaking the waves

Maslov believed that with enough organisation a side could outnumber the opposition in every part of the pitch, an idea one Kyiv journalist suspected he had borrowed from basketball. That needed a fixed point so the full-backs could step up when required. He found it in the veteran defender Vasyl Turyanchyk, stationed in front of the back four as the first holding midfielder in Soviet football, much as Zakariás had played for Hungary. His job was to break the waves of opposing attacks and to start Dynamo's own, and his knack for stepping out of the line with cover behind him was the seed from which the pressing game grew.

Hunting in packs

Once Turyanchyk could step out safely, Maslov taught the rest to follow. By the 1966 title season Dynamo's midfield was hunting in packs, closing down the man in possession and winning the ball in areas nobody had contested before. Moscow was appalled; one newspaper printed a photograph of four Dynamo players converging on a single opponent under a sneering caption. Pressing demands constant movement, so it required full-time professionals and modern conditioning. Volodymyr Muntyan insists it was Maslov, not Lobanovskyi, who first put physical preparation at the heart of Dynamo's training, working on instinct where his disciple would later use science.

“We don't need this kind of football.”
Dynamo Kyiv   Opponent in possession

Eleven in thirty-six

The numbers ended the argument. Dynamo had conceded forty-eight goals in each of the two seasons before Maslov arrived. In his first year that fell to twenty-nine, then twenty-two, then seventeen in the 1966 title season and just eleven in thirty-six games as they retained the championship in 1967. A third straight title followed in 1968. The system was not unbeatable: Lobanovskyi's Shakhtar beat them 2-1 by man-marking Muntyan and Szabo and attacking with four forwards, and Górnik Zabrze's pace knocked them out of the European Cup. But this was a machine with more than one gear, able to switch from a technical, southern style to a direct one with a single substitution.

The aeroplane

Having cut the forward line from four to two, Maslov predicted that one day sides would field a single striker. Szabo remembered the 4-4-2 as only a formal order: in play there was complete interchangeability, defenders pressing forward knowing a team-mate would cover, forwards dropping into midfield while midfielders took their places, a prototype of Total Football that Western Europe never saw. Dynamo slipped to seventh in 1970 and the club sacked him in a Moscow hotel before a match; on the way to the airport the team bus stopped at a metro station and dropped Grandad off. He died in 1977. No coach since, Wilson concludes, has been so influential.

“Football is like an aeroplane… you have to make the head more streamlined.”
