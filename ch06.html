Chapter 6

The Hungarian Connection

Wembley, 25 November 1953. The Aranycsapat – Hungary’s Golden Squad, Olympic champions, unbeaten in three years – visit the mother of football and win 6–3. Tom Finney, watching from the press-box, called it ‘cart-horses playing race-horses’. The cart-horses had a number for every position. The race-horses had a No. 9 who refused to stand where a No. 9 should.

The W-M and the bull at the gate

By the 1940s the W-M had beaten 2-3-5 in almost every mind in Europe, and it had bred a particular kind of centre-forward. With a stopper centre-half hanging round his neck, the dribbler and the darter got dominated, so managers turned to the battering ram: Arsenal’s Ted Drake, ‘strong, powerful, brave and almost entirely unthinking’ – ‘the brainless bull at the gate’, in Glanville’s phrase.

Hungary had a problem. Beefy target-men were thin on the ground in Budapest, where football, as in Vienna, was a matter for intellectual debate. Either the country started breeding English No. 9s, or somebody had to build a system that kept the W-M’s solidity without needing one.

Bukovi inverts the W

Márton Bukovi, the perpetually grumpy coach of MTK (Vörös Lobogó after nationalisation in 1949) and ‘arguably the greatest tactician of the twentieth century’, found the answer after his ‘tank’, Norbert Höfling, was sold to Lazio in 1948. If you don’t have the right centre-forward, don’t force the wrong man into the position: do away with him altogether. He inverted the W of the W-M into what was effectively an M-M.

‘The centre-forward was having increasing difficulties with a marker around his neck. So the idea emerged to play the No. 9 deeper where there was some space.’ – Nándor Hidegkuti

At MTK the No. 9 shirt went to the wing-half Péter Palotás, who never had a hard shot and was never asked to score. He collected passes from his defence and fed his wingers and inside-forwards, who pushed on to become a fluid front four. Because his play clashed with the wing-halves, one of them withdrew to defend and the other linked with him as a midfield forager.

From Palotás to Hidegkuti

Gusztáv Sebes – a national manager whose genius lay less in tactical acumen than in organisation and his knack for negotiating the endless Communist committees – took Bukovi’s system to the national team, and took Palotás with it. Hungary won Olympic gold in 1952 with Palotás withdrawn and Hidegkuti mostly on the right wing.

That September, 2–0 down in a friendly against Switzerland, Sebes swapped them. Hungary came back to win 4–2, and Hidegkuti, already thirty, made the position his own. ‘Withdrawn centre-forward’ was always a misnomer derived from his shirt number; in modern terms he was simply an attacking midfielder.

‘He was perfect for the role, sitting at the front of midfield, making telling passes, dragging the opposition defence out of shape and making fantastic runs to score himself.’ – Ferenc Puskás

Sebes’s notebook

Fluidity is easy; keeping a shape to defend with is the hard part, and that was where Sebes excelled. He had the team train with the heavier English balls on a pitch marked out to Wembley’s dimensions, and his notebook sketched the plan. The full-backs Buzánszky and Lantos were to advance, so Gyula Lóránt dropped deeper, into something like the sweeper of Karl Rappan’s verrou. Bozsik, notionally right-half, pushed on to support Hidegkuti; Zakariás, the left-half, sat so deep he was almost between the two full-backs. The wingers dropped a little to pick up passes, and Puskás and Kocsis played closer to goal than any inside-forwards in a W-M.

Two full-backs, two central defensive presences, two players running the middle and four up front: the Hungarian system was a hair’s-breadth from 4-2-4.

Wembley, 25 November 1953

England’s players had grown up in a culture where the number denoted the position: the 7 lined up against the 3, the 5 took care of the 9. Kenneth Wolstenholme felt obliged to warn his viewers early on that the Hungarians ‘number the players rather logically, with the centre-half as 3 and the backs as 2 and 4’. That morning Frank Coles had assured the Daily Telegraph that ‘Hungary’s superb ball-jugglers can be checked by firm tackling’. Wolstenholme was enraptured when Puskás did half a dozen keepie-ups waiting for the kick-off.

‘It was because of tactics that Hungary won. The match showed the clash of two formations and, as often happens, the newer, more developed formation prevailed.’ – Jenő Buzánszky, right-back

Harry Johnston’s dilemma

What was a centre-half to do when the centre-forward kept disappearing towards the halfway line? If Johnston followed Hidegkuti, he left a hole between the full-backs for Puskás and Kocsis to run into. If he sat off, Hidegkuti drifted around unchallenged, dictating the play. Caught between the two stools, Johnston watched Hidegkuti score inside the first minute and finish with a hat-trick.

‘To me the tragedy was the utter helplessness… being unable to do anything to alter the grim outlook.’ – Harry Johnston

Syd Owen replaced him for the rematch in Budapest six months later and fared no better. England lost 7–1. It was, Owen said, ‘like playing people from outer space’.

Flitting red spirits

England, wrote Geoffrey Green in The Times, ‘found themselves strangers in a strange world, a world of flitting red spirits’. Bukovi’s idea suited Hungary’s forwards perfectly: four front men and a withdrawn No. 9 gave the attack a fluidity no marking scheme could pin down. Midway through the first half Wolstenholme observed, somewhere between amusement and amazement, that ‘the outside-left Czibor came across to pick up the ball in the outside-right position’. Puskás had licence to roam; Bozsik surged up to the edge of the penalty area and scored.

Sebes, whose Communist credentials were impeccable, called it a victory for socialism: the interplay of a team against the dissociated individuality of England. As the flags hung limp in the fog above the Twin Towers, it was not hard to read the evening as Empire’s symbolic defeat.

The porous back three

The more fluid a team, the harder it is to keep the structures needed to defend, and Hungary’s defence was porous even by the standards of the day. They leaked eleven goals in the six-game run that ended at Wembley and ten in the 1954 World Cup. Everyone agreed England’s three flattered them; it could just as well be read as a criticism of Hungarian laxity.

Three at the back operates on a pivot: the far full-back tucks in beside the centre-back when the attack comes down the other flank. A smart cross-field ball ‘turns’ it, and at the very least gives the opposite winger acceleration room. Zakariás, still notionally a midfielder, did not sit deep enough to let his full-back stay tight.

Acceleration room for the far winger

Berne, 4 July 1954

After thirty-six games unbeaten, the Aranycsapat threw away a two-goal lead and lost the World Cup final 3–2 to West Germany: ill luck, a muddy pitch that hampered the passing game, a touch of complacency, and Sepp Herberger’s simple ploy of sitting Horst Eckel man-to-man on Hidegkuti. A system thought up to free the centre-forward from the clutches of a marker fell down when the marker was moved closer to him.

The team that had come home from Wembley to adoring crowds had to be diverted to Tatabánya to avoid street demonstrations. Puskás was barracked, Sebes’s son was beaten up at school, the goalkeeper Grosics was arrested, and by 1956 Sebes had been replaced by a five-man committee headed by Bukovi.

The idea travels

The Uprising and its suppression brought the defections of Puskás, Czibor and Kocsis and the entire Under-21 squad. With MTK hollowed out by the Holocaust and Ferencváros deliberately run down by the Communists, the two great well-springs had dried; Hungarian football has never recovered.

The shape survived. Béla Guttmann, the last of the coffee-house coaches, took Honvéd on tour to Brazil during the Uprising, stayed on at São Paulo and claimed to have exported the Hungarian 4-2-4 – though the truth owes more to two other Hungarian émigrés, Imre Hirschl and Dori Kürschner. At Benfica he dropped Mario Coluna deeper to make room for Eusébio, ‘Benfica’s Hidegkuti’, and won two European Cups. The man who took his mantle was Helenio Herrera, who preferred conceding one fewer to scoring one more. Next: how Brazil made the 4-2-4 its own.
