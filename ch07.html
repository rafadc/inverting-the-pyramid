Chapter 7

Harnessing the Carnival

Brazil came to the W-M late and left it early. Between the 8–4 humiliation in Belgrade in 1934 and the chants of ‘Samba, samba’ in Sweden in 1958, a football culture built on jeitinho and the trickery of the malandro went looking for a framework that gave improvisation room to breathe while sparing the defence the exposure poor Bigode had suffered. This is how a tilted W-M became the 4-2-4.

Space between the lines

Brazilian football began as the systema ingleza of Scottish expats and the tabelinha of Archie McLean, but in street kickabouts with balls made of rags a different game grew up: individual, impertinent, uninhibited about showing off. McLean thought the locals ‘terribly undisciplined’. In 1934 a Brazil side containing Leônidas, Domingos da Guia and Waldemar de Brito went to Belgrade and lost 8–4.

‘There was a lot of space between the lines. The Yugoslavs were able to exploit that, showing up the faults of the old-fashioned system.’ — Ivan Soter
Brazil in the 2-3-5 a Yugoslav runner

Kürschner’s W-M was really a W-W

Gentil Cardoso, a black former bootblack and merchant seaman who claimed to have watched Chapman build the W-M at Arsenal, preached it first; nobody took him seriously. It took Dori Kürschner, Jimmy Hogan’s successor at MTK, brought to Flamengo in 1937, to implant it. His centre-half Fausto dos Santos, ‘the Black Wonder’, refused to drop back and was fined by the president. But Kürschner’s W-M was Danubian: no Herbie Roberts stopper, the centre-half behind his half-backs yet in front of the full-backs, closer to Pozzo’s metodo. Flávio Costa, his assistant, undermined him at every turn, and after one defeat at the new Gávea stadium he was sacked.

The shaded square is the heart of the W-M: two half-backs, two inside-forwards.

Flávio Costa’s diagonal

Kürschner’s replacement did not go back to the 2-3-5. He nudged the square at the centre of the W-M until it became a parallelogram. Three backs and three forwards stayed, but the right-half, Volante, sat deep while the left-half, Jayme, advanced; the inside-right, Zizinho, dropped a little so the inside-left, Perácio, could play as the ponta da lança, the point of the lance. By 1949 the diagonal had Ademir as a roaming number 9, Jair as the lance and Zizinho probing from deep. Brazil scored 39 goals in seven games to win the Copa América, beating Paraguay 7–0 in the play-off.

‘Once the square has become a parallelogram, it doesn’t take much more of a nudge for it to become a diamond, and when that has happened, what is left is 4-2-4.’
Brazil 1949–50: Bauer deep, Danilo up; Zizinho deep, Jair up.

1950: the diagonal abandoned

Zizinho was injured for the opening 4–0 over Mexico, three Paulista midfielders were parachuted in for a 2–2 with Switzerland’s verrou, and a shaken Flávio Costa switched to an orthodox W-M for the last four games, with both half-backs, Danilo and Carlos Bauer, deeper. Brazil beat Yugoslavia, then Sweden 7–1 and Spain 6–1: ‘the football of the future’. Uruguay were still playing a metodo with Obdulio Varela as a ball-playing centre-half, but Varela had learned new ways of defending from the Hungarian Imre Hirschl. Matías González stayed deep as a sweeper, Eusebio Tejera became a centre-back, Gambetta and Andrade man-marked Chico and Friaça. It was Rappan’s 1-3-3-3 in all but name. Danilo could not help himself but push forward, and Bigode, now an orthodox left-back, was left with a fatal space in front of him.

Brazil in the W-M Uruguay’s verrou. The red patch is the room Ghiggia found.

Ghiggia, twice

Officially 173,850 were in the Maracanã; probably more than 200,000. O Mundo had printed Brazil’s photograph under ‘These are the world champions’; Varela bought every copy in his hotel and had his team-mates urinate on them. Friaça scored two minutes after half-time. Twenty-four minutes from the end Varela spread the ball right to Ghiggia, who checked as Bigode came, surged past him and crossed low for Schiaffino at the near post. ‘Silence in the Maracanã,’ said Flávio Costa, ‘which terrified our players.’ Thirteen minutes later Ghiggia laid the ball back to Julio Pérez, who slipped a return behind Bigode, and with Barbosa anticipating a cross the bobbling shot went in at the near post. Bigode, Barbosa and Juvenal, Brazil’s three black players, were blamed for the rest of their lives.

‘The last four games of the World Cup were the first time in my life I played W-M… Uruguay didn’t play W-M. Uruguay played with one deep back and the other in front.’ — Zizinho

An English clergyman for an Argentinian dancer

Twenty-two goals scored in six games did not matter; the two conceded at the last did. For 1954 the attack-minded Flávio Costa gave way to the cautious Zezé Moreira, the great trio of inside-forwards to a stopper centre-half, Pinheiro, and Brazil went out to Hungary in the Battle of Berne. Yet Zezé Moreira’s lasting contribution was made at Fluminense: zonal marking, which did away with the strict man-to-man marking that had failed so disastrously in 1950 and let defenders attack ‘from all positions’. From here the eleven wear the names of Feola’s 1958 side.

Brazil, now in yellow. Each defender owns a zone, not a man.

Square, parallelogram, diamond

‘It has many fathers,’ says Roberto Assaf of the 4-2-4: Zezé Moreira; the Paraguayan Manuel Fleitas Solich, who won three Carioca titles with it at Flamengo from 1953; Lula at Santos; perhaps even Boris Arkadiev at Dinamo Moscow. The first to use it consciously seems to have been Martim Francisco at Vila Nova, a club from Nova Lima near Belo Horizonte. He pushed his left-half, Lito, back as the quarto zagueiro, the fourth defender, and had his right-winger, Osório, drop in so a two-man midfield would not be swamped. Watch Flávio Costa’s nudge finish its work: Orlando falls back to the line, Zito and Didi become the midfield two, Pelé stays the point of the lance.

The shaded shape follows Zito, Orlando, Pelé and Didi.

The fourth defender and the flying full-back

Given the space in front of them, the 4-2-4 encouraged full-backs to advance while providing immediate cover: once marking was zonal, the fourth defender simply held his position when the full-back went, and the side kept the three-man cover it would have had in the W-M. Djalma Santos and Nílton Santos could go; Orlando stayed. Arsenal, touring in 1949, had seen the habit and thought it indiscipline.

‘Suddenly, a bloke comes dashing through and he’s had a shot at goal… We found out it was their full-back. See, they didn’t care. I never went up there like that.’ — Laurie Scott
The shaded band is the cover that remains when Nílton Santos goes.

Guttmann’s ‘tat-tat-tat’

When Béla Guttmann’s Honvéd toured in November 1956 their shape surprised nobody; Brazil was further down the road to 4-2-4 than Hungary. ‘The only difference,’ said Hidegkuti, ‘was in the number worn by the forward who withdrew into midfield.’ What Guttmann brought to São Paulo in 1957 was purpose: a grid painted on a wall, long balls for the centre-forward to flick to the wingers, no dallying, ‘ping-pang-pong’. He signed the 34-year-old Zizinho as the creative half of the midfield two and dropped Dino Sani to sit behind him. ‘It was only then,’ Zizinho said, ‘that I really began to play.’ São Paulo won the Paulista title, Guttmann left for Europe, and his assistant, Vicente Feola, inherited the template.

Zito sits, Didi creates: the volante and the playmaker.

‘The first pass goes to Garrincha’

Feola, the jovial fat man the press said slept on the bench, ran the best-prepared squad in Brazil’s history: a dentist who pulled 470 teeth, a spy, and a psychologist who called Pelé ‘obviously infantile’ and judged Garrincha unfit to drive a bus in São Paulo. Neither played in the 3–0 over Austria, where the disciplined Joel tracked back on Ernesto Santos’s warning about Austria’s four midfielders, nor against England. Both started against the USSR. ‘Remember,’ Feola told Didi, ‘the first pass goes to Garrincha.’ Within twenty seconds Garrincha had put Kuznetsov on the ground, then again, then Voinov, and hit the post; Pelé hit the bar; Vavá scored from Didi’s through-ball. Gabriel Hanot reckoned no three minutes of football had ever been better.

Brazil’s 4-2-4 USSR

England sit on Didi

Bill Nicholson scouted Brazil and concluded the way to stop them was to stop Didi. Walter Winterbottom, in a move almost without precedent for England, changed his side to counter an opponent: Don Howe came in as a second centre-half beside Billy Wright, Tommy Banks and Eddie Clamp attacked from full-back, and Bill Slater was deputed to sit tight on Didi. Vavá hit the bar, Clamp cleared off the line, Colin McDonald saved twice from Altafini, and England got away with a goalless draw. Backroom staff on both sides: the game was professionalising everywhere.

England, with Slater shadowing Didi.

Zagallo drops: 4-2-4 becomes 4-3-3

In practice the 4-2-4 almost never appeared as a 4-2-4. In possession it was a 3-3-4, the fourth defender stepping up; out of possession a 4-3-3. Feola said it was Zagallo’s role, balancing the anarchic brilliance of Garrincha, that proved the key: a converted inside-forward tracking back and forth on the left, doing what Osório had done for Vila Nova. Wales fell 1–0, France 5–2, Sweden 5–2 in the final. ‘There was no doubt this time,’ wrote Glanville, ‘that the best, immeasurably the finest, team had won.’

The shaded corridor is Zagallo’s. The ball shows who has possession.

Chile 1962: the last triumph of the winger

By 1962 Zagallo played so deep the system was simply called 4-3-3. Aimoré Moreira, who replaced the ailing Feola, explained it by age: Didi ‘more and more a player good at waiting in midfield’, Zito ‘quicker, more dynamic, able to shuttle backwards and forwards’. Pelé was injured after two games; Garrincha was enough. Opponents set two or three men on him and he simply bypassed them: two goals against England, two more and a red card against Chile, reprieved for the final. ‘Tactics,’ Guttmann once said of his own untameable winger Canhoteiro, ‘are for everybody, but they are not valid for him.’

Markers converge on Garrincha. Amarildo takes Pelé’s place.

The player faster than the ball

‘For the Englishman, football is an athletic exercise; for the Brazilian it’s a game… English football requires that the ball moves faster than the player; Brazilian football requires that the player be faster than the ball. The English player thinks; the Brazilian improvises.’ — Tomas Mazzoni, 1949

The virtue of a back four was that it did not depend on players of that calibre, as Viktor Maslov and Alf Ramsey would shortly demonstrate, yet it gave such players a setting in which to flourish. Everyone else caught on quickly. When the 1966 World Cup came round, the W-M was already a museum piece.
