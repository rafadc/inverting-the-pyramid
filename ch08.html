Chapter 8

The English Pragmatism (1)

Birmingham Airport, March 1960. Helenio Herrera's Barcelona have just put nine past Wolves, the English champions, and he tells the press that England still play the way the continent did years ago: strength without method. This chapter explains why — the muddle after Hungary, Stan Cullis's long game, Charles Reep's notebook — and how Alf Ramsey turned an idea about three passes into a World Cup.

Tea at five

The two thrashings by Hungary had killed the myth of English superiority, and everyone agreed something had to change. Nobody agreed on what. The W-M took the blame yet stayed the default at almost every club, and the loudest alternative, Willy Meisl's Soccer Revolution, was to go backwards to the 2-3-5 of the golden age. Herrera's mockery stung because it was accurate: while the rest of the world grew more sophisticated, England dithered between a shape it distrusted and one that had looked outmoded twenty years earlier.

The English are creatures of habit: tea at five.
England, W-M then 2-3-5 and back

The Revie Plan

Only Manchester City seriously tried to copy Hungary. Johnny Williamson had prospered as a deep-lying centre-forward in the reserves, so for 1954–55 Les McDowall handed the role to Don Revie in the first team. After a 5–0 opening-day hiding at Preston the idea settled: City reached the Cup final, finished seventh and Revie was named Footballer of the Year. Then it unravelled. A row over an unsanctioned holiday, a move to Sunderland where every opponent knew the trick, and finally Leeds, who used him as an ordinary inside-forward. The experiment's momentum was gone.

Don's system was alright in Manchester, but everybody knew about it.
Manchester City Opponents

Push and run

The real radicalism was at Tottenham, where Peter McWilliam's Scottish passing game passed down to Arthur Rowe. Rowe, who had lectured in Hungary in 1939, built a side on fifteen- to twenty-yard passes and on never holding the ball longer than necessary. His first signing was Alf Ramsey, an attacking right-back told to stop hitting long balls and instead feed a withdrawn winger, Sonny Walters, while Bill Nicholson sat in to cover. Spurs won the Second Division in 1950 and the First in 1951, a wave of triangles and squares that made the Telegraph swoon.

A good player runs to the ball. A bad player runs after it.
Tottenham Opponents

Champions of the World

Under the new Molineux floodlights Stan Cullis's Wolves beat Racing, Dinamo and Spartak, then on 14 December 1954 hosted the Honvéd of Puskás, Kocsis, Bozsik and Czibor. Cullis had apprentices, among them a sixteen-year-old Ron Atkinson, water an already sodden pitch. Honvéd led 2–0 before the mud swallowed their tricks; at half-time Cullis ordered longer passes to get behind full-backs he thought were too square, and Wilshaw's crosses gave Swinbourne two goals in ninety seconds. The Daily Mail crowned them champions of the world, a claim that so annoyed Gabriel Hanot he founded the European Cup to disprove it.

Quagmires are not usually considered the best pitches.
Wolves Honvéd

Cullis reads the 6–3

Where others saw the Wembley 6–3 as a festival of passing, Cullis saw proof of his own creed. Grosics cleared long. Only one Hungarian goal came from a move that began in their own half; three came from moves of a single pass, one from two passes, one from a free-kick. The admired keep-ball, he noted, only arrived once Hungary were protecting a lead. His logic was blunt: the number of chances is proportional to the time the ball spends in front of goal, so defenders should clear early and attackers should not dawdle. Alf Ramsey, England's right-back that afternoon, drew the same conclusion.

The Wing-Commander's notebook

Charles Reep, an RAF officer converted by Charles Jones's 1933 lectures on Chapman's Arsenal, lost patience at Swindon's County Ground on 18 March 1950 (he later wrote 19 March, a day with no game). Watching attack after attack come to nothing, he began recording them: 147 in the second half alone. Extrapolating to 280 attacks and two goals a game, he arrived at a 99.29 per cent failure rate; improve it by 0.71 points and you would average three goals and win promotion. Brentford hired him part-time in 1951 and their goals per game doubled. From 1953 to 1967 he and Bernard Benjamin of the Royal Statistical Society logged 578 matches.

Three passes or fewer

Reep's figures said only two goals in nine came from moves of more than three received passes, that a long pass from your own half made a move more effective, and that roughly eight shots produced a goal. He mapped a Position of Maximum Opportunity near the back post and told wingers to wait high on the touchline for the long ball, head for the near post, and make for the far post when the other flank had it. Wilson's objection is arithmetical: 91.5 per cent of all moves were three passes or fewer but only around 80 per cent of goals, so the rarer long moves were the more productive ones.

Common does not necessarily equal good.
Reep's direct attack, then the share of moves and of goals

Leadbetter drops deep

Ramsey took over Third Division South Ipswich in 1955 knowing push-and-run would not work there. His decisive move came at New Year: Jimmy Leadbetter, a slow, clever inside-forward, was posted to the left wing and told to collect the ball from his own defenders. Full-backs would not follow him that deep; when they did, they left a hole that Ted Phillips and later Ray Crawford ran into. It was 4-2-4 with a twist, closer in shape to Brazil's lopsided 4-3-3 of 1962, and it won the title in 1962 on a £30,000 squad, until Bill Nicholson's Spurs read it and won the Charity Shield 5–1.

If I had to suggest an ideal number of passes, I would say three.
Ipswich Town Fulham (Cohen at right-back)

The winger is finished

Ramsey's rethink followed a 1964 tour of South America: a 5–1 defeat to Brazil, then a 1–0 loss to an Argentina who sat deep, kept the ball and let England pass to nowhere. 4-2-4, he decided, was potent when you had possession but useless for winning it back. Wales manager Dave Bowen explained the deeper shift. Against three defenders the far full-back covered behind the centre-half, so a winger always had room to receive a cross-field pass. With four at the back, the full-back could stand tight and take the winger's acceleration space away. Two men stuck out wide became a luxury.

A luxury which can virtually leave a side with nine men.
Attack Defence, three backs then four

Wingless Wonders

Walter Winterbottom had picked England alongside a committee; Ramsey demanded control, and after the committee's W-M lost 5–2 in Paris he got his 4-2-4. In February 1965 a senior XI in 4-3-3 destroyed the Under-23s, and Nobby Stiles, Alan Ball and finally Martin Peters arrived. By the Argentina quarter-final in 1966 the side was, as Stiles put it, a 4-1-3-2: Stiles anchoring, Ball, Charlton and Peters breaking, Hunt and Hurst ahead. The fourth goal of the final, Moore's long pass and Hurst's thump, was exactly the kind Ramsey had loved at Ipswich, though England could pass when they chose. Sacked in 1974, he had no regrets.

I'm employed to win football matches. That's all.
England West Germany
