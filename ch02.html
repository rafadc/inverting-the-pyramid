Chapter 2

The Waltz and the Tango

Wherever the British went to trade, they left football behind. Vienna and Budapest, Buenos Aires and Montevideo all kept the 2-3-5 pyramid, but the people who took it up had never been near an English public school and saw no reason to play it the English way. From one old shape grew two new styles: one light and easy, one born in the slums.

Football follows the money

By the 1880s a fifth of Britain's foreign investment was in South America and 45,000 Britons lived around Buenos Aires. Wherever there was an embassy, a bank or a railway there was soon a football club: Újpest in Budapest in 1885; the Vienna Cricket Club against Baron Rothschild's gardeners in 1894; Alexander Watson Hutton's English High School in Buenos Aires; William Leslie Poole's Albion in Montevideo.

What they exported was the 2-3-5, complete. The shape was never in question. The style would be.

The British 2-3-5, as shipped

Imitation and invention

Anglophile Danes, Dutch and Swedes copied faithfully. Denmark took silver at the 1908 Olympics, and Dutch club photographs of the age are ‘a pastiche of Victorian Englishness, all drooping moustaches and studied indifference’. The aim was to play ‘on English grounds, with all their English customs and English strategies… amid the beautiful Dutch landscape’.

It was in central Europe and South America, where the British were regarded with more scepticism, that the game began to evolve. Both kept the pyramid. Where Britain persisted in ruggedness and physicality, others developed subtler forms of the game.

Jimmy Hogan, the Parson

The greatest teacher of the Scottish game was an Englishman of Irish descent from Burnley. Nicknamed ‘the Parson’ for his ascetic habits, Hogan was baffled by a football culture that frowned on training with the ball: Tottenham's 1904 schedule had two ball sessions a week, on the theory that a player starved of it would be hungrier on Saturday.

At Dordrecht in 1910 he coached amateur students as he thought professionals should be coached. Ball control came first, then ‘the old Scottish game’ played ‘in an intelligent, constructive and progressive, on-the-carpet manner’. Tactics moved off the pitch and on to a blackboard.

A night in Vienna, 1912

Austria drew 1–1 with Hungary in a game refereed by Hogan's friend James Howcroft. Hugo Meisl, a banker who had become the de facto head of the Austrian federation, asked where his side was going wrong. Howcroft told him he needed a coach, and Hogan arrived on a six-week contract. His first session baffled the players; Meisl was enthralled, and the two talked long into the night.

Neither saw anything wrong with the 2-3-5. What they wanted was movement, because too many teams were rigid and therefore predictable. Make the ball do the work, control an incoming pass instantly and release it, and use the long pass to unsettle a defence, ‘provided it was well directed and not an aimless upfield punt’. Hogan said Austrian football was like a waltz, ‘light and easy’.

The Danubian School

War interned Hogan, but Meisl kept the template. Its sternest test came on a frozen, rutted pitch in Nuremberg soon after the war, where Southern Germany won 5–0 and the close-passing game proved impractical. On the train home a despondent Meisl asked his players whether to go direct and physical. Absolutely not, they said, and the principles were set in stone.

From them grew the Wunderteam of the early thirties, ‘the first of the great unfulfilled national sides’: still a pyramid, but with the centre-forward (Matthias Sindelar, whose story waits for Chapter 4) dropping away from the front line to pull the strings. Technique was prized over physicality, but harnessed to a team structure. Under Meisl, wrote Brian Glanville, ‘soccer became almost an exhibition, a sort of competitive ballet, in which scoring goals was no more than the excuse for the weaving of a hundred intricate patterns’.

Budapest: ‘They are mine’

Arrested as an enemy alien in 1914, Hogan was bailed by the Blythe brothers, department-store owners whose children he taught tennis. Eighteen months later the MTK director Edward Shires and Baron Dirstay pulled diplomatic strings to bring him to Budapest, provided he reported regularly to the police. With the senior players at the front he built a side from schoolboys, spotting György Orth and József ‘Csibi’ Braun in a kickabout in Angol Park: ‘I pounced on them and said “they are mine, my very own”.’

MTK won the 1916–17 title and held it for nine years. Hogan's successor, Dori Kürschner, would carry the ideas to Brazil. Hogan went home to a dispatch job at a tobacco firm and, when he asked the FA for hardship money, its secretary Frederick Wall handed him three pairs of khaki socks. He never forgave them, and his talent was lost to England.

‘Football blossomed in the slums’

On the River Plate the British schools laid the same foundation. Hutton's old boys, Alumni, dominated the early Argentine league; the ideal was ‘to play well without passion’, and Alumni once refused to take a penalty they thought wrongly given. Then the AFA switched to Spanish in 1903, Alumni folded in 1911, and the game passed to the sons of Italian and Spanish immigrants who had ‘no similar sense of physicality as a virtue in its own right, no similar distrust of cunning’.

‘Like the tango, football blossomed in the slums… Dancers drew filigrees on a single floor tile, and football players created their own language in that tiny space where they chose to retain and possess the ball rather than kick it, as if their feet were hands braiding the leather.’ — Eduardo Galeano

Eights and tens drop back

The touring British hated it. Nottingham Forest's shoulder-charging soured their 1905 visit; Swindon's manager Samuel Allen, otherwise admiring, worried in 1912 that locals ‘look on individual exploits as the main thing’. Even Jorge Brown of Alumni complained that the new game ‘was weakened by an excess of passing close to the goal… more fine, perhaps more artistic, even apparently more intelligent, but it has lost its primitive enthusiasm’.

The shape stayed a 2-3-5, gently tinkered with. ‘It was the era when we had five forwards with the number 8 and the number 10 dropping back and wingers sending in passes,’ said Francisco Varallo, Argentina's inside-right in the first World Cup final. The inside-forwards became the creative hub, and the game grew a cult of the gambeta, the slaloming dribble.

Ants and cicadas

Uruguay went to the 1924 Olympics as a team of workers, a meat-packer, a marble-cutter and an ice salesman among them, travelling in steerage and winning nine friendlies in Spain to pay their way. In Paris they beat Yugoslavia 7–0, scored seventeen, conceded two and took gold. ‘Those men, slippery as squirrels, who played chess with a ball,’ wrote Galeano. Comparing them with Britain, said Gabriel Hanot, was ‘like comparing Arab thoroughbreds to farm horses’.

Argentina lost to them in the 1928 Olympic final and again in the first World Cup final in 1930. The difference, as far as the reports allow, was that Uruguay kept a defensive shape while Argentine individualism dissolved into confusion. ‘The ants are the Uruguayans,’ wrote Gianni Brera, ‘the cicadas are the Argentinians.’ Uruguay called their grit la garra charrúa. The whole history of tactics, Wilson says, is the struggle to balance defensive solidity with attacking fluidity.

‘Yes, but I'm not having fun’

Shut out of the world game after 1934, when four Argentinians helped Italy win the World Cup, and then isolated under Perón, Argentina spent twenty years perfecting la nuestra, ‘our style’, rooted in the criolla viveza, native cunning. Between September 1936 and April 1938 there was not one goalless draw in the championship. Players were lionised as inventors of tricks: Evaristo the volleyed backheel, Bartolucci the diving header, Calomino, perhaps, the bicycle-kick.

Ernesto Sabato's anecdote about two Independiente inside-forwards says everything. At half-time Seoane asks Lalín to cross so he can score. Lalín crosses, Seoane scores and runs back shouting, ‘See, Lalín, see?!’ Lalín answers: ‘Yes, but I'm not having fun.’ There you have, says Sabato's narrator, ‘the whole problem of Argentinian football’.
